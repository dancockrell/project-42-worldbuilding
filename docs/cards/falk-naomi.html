Project 42 · Officer · S tier

CAPTAIN (DR) NAOMI FALK

Signs the only document that says whether a man who has come back is still,
in any sense the programme has a form for, reliably present, and has not,
since a conversation with a settled physician in June that she has told
nobody about, believed one word of what the document's own tables instruct
her to write.

She signs anyway, on the settled arithmetic that a camp with one doctor who
refuses to sign is a camp shortly to have a different doctor who will, and
she has done that arithmetic in full, more than once, in the particular
hours when it is hardest not to.

THE TABLES

Halloran-Sze had read the coherence tables in her first week at the camp,
before she had unpacked, before she had learned where the mess hall was,
sitting at the small table in her quarters with the appendix open in front
of her and a cup of tea going cold beside it, and had said, when Falk
asked, some weeks later, what she made of them, that they were written to
be signed rather than to be true, a distinction Halloran-Sze had delivered
in the same flat clinical register she used for everything and had clearly
expected Falk to find as unremarkable as she plainly found it herself, a
workhouse physician's habit of speaking the worst thing plainly having
survived, evidently, a century and a half and one retrieval intact.

Falk had not found it unremarkable. She had turned the observation over
for the better part of a year, on nights when a condition code did not
seem to match the man standing in front of her, and had told herself,
each time, that Halloran-Sze had been speaking generally, about
institutions and their instruments, and not specifically about the bands
Falk herself was required to initial every time an agent returned from
somewhere the century had already lost him — a distinction that had held,
more or less, until the February morning she found Halloran-Sze dead in
her bed of a stroke, fifty-four years old, the notebook still on the
table where she had left it, containing, among a great many other
observations Falk had not yet finished reading, a full page on the tables
that amounted to a diagnosis rather than a complaint.

Falk had not filed the notebook. She had told herself, in the days
immediately after, that there had been no urgent occasion to, and had
gone on telling herself some version of that for six months, through
spring and into June, when Reyes-Whitlock had come in for his eleventh
assessment and she had watched him answer her questions in the same
patient, slightly-too-slow voice he always used, and had written, on the
form, the code the tables instructed her to write, 3-C, satisfactory,
minor coherence variance, no action indicated, and had known, entering it,
in the specific and total way one knows a fact about one's own hand
rather than about the world, that it was not true, and had signed it
anyway, because the alternative to a physician who signs falsely is not a
physician who signs truly, there being no other physician within reach of
this installation who would sign at all, and a request for a second
medical officer had already been filed, in April, and denied, on grounds
of cost, and she had not resubmitted it, for reasons she had not
examined.

She still has Halloran-Sze's notebook. She has never filed it, and has
stopped, sometime around March, telling herself that she intends to, and
sits with it some evenings, in the acoustically dead quiet of the mess
hall after the others have gone, reading the same page about the tables
that she has by now effectively memorised, as though rereading it might
eventually produce a course of action other than the one she has already,
demonstrably, chosen.
