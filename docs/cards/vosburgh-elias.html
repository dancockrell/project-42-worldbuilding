Project 42 · Officer · A tier

MAJOR ELIAS VOSBURGH

Outranks, on every document that matters, a clerk with no service record
he can point to and no authority beyond a desk, and has never once, in
four years, given her an order she did not already intend to follow,
which he understands, some nights better than others, is not the same
thing as an order she would obey.

He is the officer who runs Camp Iron Bell, in the sense that his name is
on the roster, and he has arranged, without ever discussing it with
anyone, to be somewhere else — Hazlehurst, usually, on business that does
not strictly require him — for the two weeks in July when she takes her
leave, having learned once, badly, what the installation does without her
and having no wish to learn it twice while present to watch it happen.

THE SECOND WEEK OF JULY

He had drafted the July roster four times before he understood what he
was actually doing, which was not scheduling the camp's work but avoiding
the two weeks in the middle of the month during which Corporal Prentiss
customarily took her leave, a fact he had known for three years and had
never once, in three years, allowed himself to arrange his own duties
around, on the grounds that a major who could not run an installation of
sixty men for a fortnight without one civilian clerk was not, properly
speaking, running it, and he had believed this about himself right up
until the one July he had tried it, the second year, when he had stayed,
on principle, and the installation had produced, over fourteen days, two
misfiled retrieval authorisations, a requisition sent to the wrong
department entirely, and a morning on which he had discovered, with a
sensation he did not care to examine too closely, that he did not know
which forms had already gone up to Wexford and which were still sitting
in the tray, a piece of information Prentiss had apparently been holding
in her head the entire time without remark, the way a woman holds the
location of a dropped pin.

He had not stayed the third year. He had found, that July, a reason to be
at Hazlehurst concerning a matter that could, on reflection, have waited,
and had returned on the Monday after her leave ended to find the
installation exactly where he had left it, which he told himself was
coincidence and which he did not, in his own private accounting, believe
for one day of the two weeks he had been gone.

What troubled him was not that she was necessary, since every installation
had somebody necessary and it was no great admission to say so; what
troubled him was the shape of the necessity, which was not the shape of
rank at all — he outranked her formally, comprehensively, on every
document the Army had ever printed, and she deferred to him in exactly
the manner the regulations required and not one particle further, and
somewhere inside that correct and unremarkable deference he had come, over
three years, to understand that he was afraid of her in a way he had
never once been afraid of Wexford, who could end his career with a
signature, because Wexford's authority was written down where Vosburgh
could see it and measure it and know its edges, and Prentiss's authority
was not written down anywhere at all, which meant he had no way of knowing
how far it went, only that it went, demonstrably, further than his own,
and that the installation had proved this to him once, in fourteen days,
without either of them ever having to discuss it.

He had drafted the fourth roster on a Sunday, alone in his office, and had
filed it as the third had been filed the year before, without comment,
and had told nobody — not Wexford, not Prentiss herself, who he suspected
knew exactly what the arrangement was and had never once acknowledged it
either — and had gone, the following Monday, to Hazlehurst, on business
that did not strictly require him, satisfied with an arrangement he could
not have defended to a board of inquiry and did not, on the whole,
propose to try.
