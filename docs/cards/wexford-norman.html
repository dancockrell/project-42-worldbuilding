COLONEL NORMAN WEXFORD

Signs every retrieval this installation has ever attempted, reads every
signature before he adds his own, and has come, over three years, to
believe without ever quite stating it to himself that the programme is
steering history rather than merely retrieving what history had already
finished with — a belief the Consistency Finding does not support and
that nobody at Camp Iron Bell has ever thought to correct him on, since
correcting him would require someone junior enough to have noticed and
senior enough to say so, and no such man has yet existed at this
installation.

He has written a letter to each person the programme has ever brought
back, sealed to be opened only in the event of his own death, and has
told no one they exist except the woman who typed the envelopes, who has
never asked what is in them and whom he has never offered to say.

THE NINETEENTH LETTER

He had written the first one the week Reyes-Whitlock arrived, in June of
the first year, sitting at his desk after the installation had gone quiet,
composing a letter to a man he had known for four days, and had found, on
finishing it, that he did not know what to do with a document that made no
sense to send and no sense to destroy, a man's welcome to an installation
he had already been living at for four days, and had settled, in the end,
on sealing it and locking it in the bottom drawer of his desk, against a
contingency he did not examine too closely, and had gone on writing one
for each person after, as they arrived, until there were nineteen, in the
same hand, in the same drawer, none of them opened, none of them
mentioned to anyone but Prentiss, who typed the envelopes without
comment and had never once, in three years, asked him what was inside
them, a silence he had come to understand as either extraordinary
discretion or a settled disinterest in a Colonel's private business, and
had decided, on balance, not to find out which, there being no way to ask
the question that would not itself constitute the telling.

He believed, without having examined the belief closely enough to notice
it needed examining, that Camp Iron Bell was accomplishing something —
that the programme reached into history and altered, in some cumulative
and difficult-to-measure way, the shape of what would otherwise have
happened, a conviction that sat comfortably beside the Consistency Finding
on his own desk, a document he had read, signed off on, and filed without
apparently connecting its central clause to the belief it directly
contradicted, that clause being the one that said, in language his own
paradox auditors used every week without incident, that nothing the
installation had ever done could have gone otherwise, that every
retrieval had always already happened, and that the nineteen people in
the Annex were not a record of history being changed but a record of
history having, from the beginning, included this installation and its
work as one more fact already settled — and nobody had told him this
plainly, in three years, partly because the men positioned to notice it
either did not think of it in those terms or thought of it and judged,
correctly, that a Colonel's operating belief was not theirs to correct
unless it produced an operational error, which it had not.

The letters remained in the drawer. He added to the count each time a
retrieval closed, in the same hand, at the same desk, and told himself
each time that the letter was for the person's own sake, a welcome they
would never read unless the worst happened, and did not examine, closely
enough to notice the examination was overdue, whether a document written
for nineteen separate people in eleven months, in identical
circumstances, at the identical hour, was in fact a record of a Colonel
teaching himself, one letter at a time, to believe in the thing he had
built.
