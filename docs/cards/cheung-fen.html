FEN CHEUNG

Forward-settled from 2004, a structural engineer who can read the load
path of a building from across a parade ground and has never once, in
either century she has lived in, needed to be told what a wall is for —
which made her first morning at Camp Iron Bell the one morning in the
camp's history that a question got asked instead of quietly answered by
everyone already knowing it.

The file records the incident as a disciplinary matter. It does not
record what she said, because the clerk who wrote the report could not
make it fit the form.

THE QUESTION WITH NO BOX FOR IT

Cheung had been at Iron Bell nine hours when she stopped in front of
Building 1 and looked, for slightly longer than a person passing a
familiar fixture would look, at the two fountains standing side by side
with a painted sign over each, and asked the corporal walking her to
Falk's office what the second one was for, given that the first one
already worked.

The corporal, a boy of nineteen from a county two over, answered her the
way he would have answered anyone asking him the time, without slowing
down, and it was only after he had finished the sentence that he heard,
for the first time in his own memory, what the sentence actually
contained, said aloud, to a person for whom it plainly explained nothing,
and he stopped walking, and Cheung stopped a step later, and neither of
them said anything else for the length of the corridor.

She did not raise her voice. She did not, in the version Falk got from
the corporal an hour later, say anything that a stenographer transcribing
it word for word would have flagged as insubordination, disrespect, or
any of the categories the disciplinary form provided a checkbox for. She
had asked one further question — whether the arrangement was written
into camp regulations or simply understood — and receiving no answer to
that one either, had gone on into Falk's office and said nothing more
about it to anyone, that day or afterward.

The corporal filed a report anyway, because something had plainly
happened and an installation like Iron Bell does not leave a plainly
happened thing unfiled, and the clerk who typed it up worked through four
drafts trying to fit what the corporal described into the form's existing
categories — conduct unbecoming, insubordination, disturbance — and
found that none of them described a woman from fifty-nine years in the
future asking, without malice and without theatre, why a wall that worked
had been built twice.

The report that survives says only that Private Cheung — her rank
misremembered, her question omitted entirely — was involved in an
incident of a non-military nature outside Building 1, and recommends no
action, there being no regulation the incident could be shown to have
broken, only one that everybody at the installation had agreed, long
before Cheung arrived to not agree with it, never to look at directly.

Wexford saw the report on his desk the next morning, read it twice, and
signed it without comment, the way he signed most things that arrived
already unable to be acted on, and did not send for Cheung, and did not
ask the corporal a single further question, and when Vosburgh raised the
county's arrangements with him some weeks later, on an entirely
unconnected matter, Wexford said, in the same flat register he used for
requisitions, that he would rather not discuss the county, and Vosburgh,
who had by then heard the corridor version of what happened outside
Building 1 at least twice, did not ask him why.
