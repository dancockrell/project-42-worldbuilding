Hyakki Yakō · Commander · S tier

ONMYŌJI-TAISA REIKO AMATSU

Wrote the nine articles of the treaty herself, over four nights, knowing
even as she drafted it that nothing had agreed to a single word — and
signs, every week, orders that depend on the fiction that it did, because
an Empire that admits it is being used has no policy, and a programme with
no policy is disbanded, and disbanding it would not stop a single
selection.

Commands thirty-eight people fused onto folklore that never consulted her
either. She is not one of them. Nothing reaches into her the way it
reaches into them, and she has never once let that difference show.

THE OFFICER WHO BELIEVES HER

Captain Ishida had asked her, more than once, when the ministry's reply to
Article VI's clarification might reasonably be expected, and each time
Amatsu had given him an answer built from the same careful materials —
patience, the difficulty of wartime post, the understandable slowness of a
distant office with larger matters on its desk — and each time he had
accepted the answer with the particular gratitude of a man being told,
gently, that the delay is ordinary, and had gone back to his post reassured,
and this had gone on now for three years, in a rhythm so steady that she
sometimes thought of it the way a farmer thinks of weather: not as a
decision she made each time but as a season that simply came around.

She had drafted Article VI herself, over the second of the four nights it
had taken her to write the whole treaty, and she had known, writing it,
exactly what she was doing — providing for the welfare of selected persons
in language sturdy enough to survive translation, notarisation, and the
scrutiny of a ministry that would never see it, because there was no
ministry to see it, because nothing had signed the other side of the
document she was filing in triplicate as though a signature existed. She
had done this with her eyes open and had never once regretted it, on the
reasoning that an Empire that admitted, in writing, to being selected
rather than selecting would have no policy left to administer, and a
programme with no policy was a programme that could be disbanded by the
next officer with an opinion, and disbanding it would not return a single
person it had already taken, or prevent it from taking the next.

What she had not accounted for, in the four nights of drafting, was Ishida.

He believed the treaty the way a man believes a bridge he has crossed every
day of his life — not examined, simply relied upon — and his faith in it
had become, over three years, indistinguishable from his faith in her,
since she was the officer who had drafted it and the officer who assured
him, twice a year, that the silence from the other side meant nothing more
than bureaucracy being bureaucracy. She had watched this happen and had
done nothing to stop it, partly because there was no way to correct one
belief without collapsing the other, and partly, she admitted to herself on
the nights she allowed such admissions, because a subordinate who believed
in her that completely was easier to command than one who did not, and she
had needed, for three years, at least one person at Light Seven who slept
soundly.

She knew what he was waiting for. She had never told him it was not
coming, and told herself this was mercy rather than convenience, and was no
longer entirely sure, four nights and three years on, that there was a
difference worth drawing between the two.
