MUSTER 4/w — "WERWOLF"

Four hundred pounds. Runs down a horse. Comes out of the kennels at
Hollernbruch with a brass plate riveted behind the ear, because the Office
labels its property. The Allies named it. The Office never has — the file says
Muster 4, variant w, and the file is not being poetic.

It takes commands by whistle at eleven hundred metres and holds a scent through
water. Nothing that has run from one has outrun one.

It does not howl. Someone removed that, deliberately, in 1939, and wrote a
paper about the removal.

THE PLATE

Gefreiter Lindt is told to walk it and he walks it.

The chain is eleven feet and he keeps nine of it, and the Muster goes ahead of
him along the dyke with its head low and its shoulders rolling, and it never
once takes the slack.

"You'll want more chain," Pflug says at the gate.

"Nine is standing orders."

"Nine is for the yard."

Lindt takes nine.

They work the east line until noon. Twice the Muster stops and Lindt stops with
it, and both times it is a hare, and both times it looks back at him and waits
to be told, and both times he says nothing and it goes on.

At the sluice the handler's whistle goes, two notes, somewhere behind them in
the reeds.

The Muster's head comes up.

It goes off the dyke into the water without a sound and it is across before
Lindt has the chain in both hands, and the chain runs out and burns his palms
and he lets it go, because a man who does not let go of eleven feet of chain
gets pulled in after it.

He finds it four hundred metres on, in the beet field, standing over a Polish
boy of about fifteen who has fallen and is not moving except to breathe.

The Muster is not touching him.

It has one forefoot on the boy's coat, pinning the cloth and nothing else, and
it is looking back down the field at Lindt, and it is waiting to be told.

The whistle goes again. Two notes. The same two.

The Muster does not move.

Lindt comes up slow and gets the chain and takes a turn round his fist, and the
animal lets him, and its ribs are going like a bellows and there is water
sheeting off it, and it has run four hundred metres in the time it takes a man
to think about running.

He looks at the boy. He looks at the brass plate behind the ear, and the number
stamped in it, and he thinks that he will have to write the number in the log,
and that he will have to write what the animal did.

He decides, standing in the beet field with the chain round his fist, that it
did not hear the second whistle. Water in the ears. It had just come out of the
sluice.

That is what he writes.

The paper goes up on Thursday with sixty others, and Section Six reads it, and
Section Six agrees. Water in the ears. A maintenance matter.

They take it back to the kennels and they look at the ears and the ears are
fine.
