YUREI-BOUND

Bound is the programme's word for it, and the file does not elaborate
past that one word, the way a form stops writing once it has found a
category that will close. Something was left unfinished, and it was not
hers to finish — she is not a ghost, whatever the folklore calls the
shape of what happened to her; she is a woman carrying an obligation she
did not incur, calmly, which is the detail that reads wrong to everyone
who meets her. The record says she volunteered. The record says that
about everyone.

THE WORD THE FORM STOPPED AT

She had read the file once, early on, when a clerk had left it turned the
wrong way on a desk and she had had thirty seconds and the nerve to use
them, and had found, under her name, the single word bound, followed by
nothing — no clause, no citation, no officer's initials taking
responsibility for having chosen that word over another — as though
whoever filled in the form had reached it, recognized it as the correct
shape, and stopped there the way a man stops digging when the shovel
finds rock, satisfied that further effort would only prove what the first
strike had already told him.

She had understood, reading it, two things at once, in the unhurried way
you understand two things that have been true for a while before you
finally look at them together. The first was that whatever had reached
into her life had left something undone behind it — a debt, a promise, a
thread she could feel the shape of without ever being shown the pattern
it belonged to — and that this unfinished thing was not hers, had never
been hers, and had been handed to her anyway the way a household hands an
unclaimed parcel to whoever happens to answer the door. The second, and
this was the one she had never said aloud to anyone, was that the word
volunteered, sitting two lines above bound on the same form, was a
lie so uniform across every file in the cabinet that it had stopped being
a lie about her specifically and become instead a fact about the
cabinet — a house style, applied the same way to everyone, true of nobody.

She served the evening line calmly, the way she did everything now, and
had noticed, over the better part of two years, that her calm was the one
thing about her that unsettled the men she fed more than anything the
word bound could have explained to them if anyone had bothered to
explain it. A woman distressed by an unclaimed obligation would have made
sense to them; grief has a shape soldiers recognize and know how to stand
near. A woman entirely composed about it read, to men trained to look for
trouble, like trouble being managed rather than trouble being absent, and
she had learned, without deciding to learn it, that this reading worked
in her favor more often than the truth would have.

She did not correct the word on the form, though she had had eleven
months and several occasions to. Correcting bound would have required
explaining what it should say instead, and she did not have a better word
than the one that had already been chosen for her by somebody following a
category he had not built and did not understand — only the private,
unspoken certainty, carried the way she carried everything now, that
whatever she was finishing on somebody's behalf, she would know it when
she reached the end of it, and that until then the ladle in her hand and
the line moving past her were as much of an answer as she required.
