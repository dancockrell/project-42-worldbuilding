ENSIGN TOBIAS FERRER

Went into the water off Finisterre in 1799 exactly once, briefly, and came
out of it a century and a half later at Camp Iron Bell, a discrepancy the
muster book has never been informed of and the muster book being, as far
as the Navy of 1799 is concerned, the only authority that matters.

He has read the entry. He keeps a fair copy of it, made from memory, in
his own hand, and produces it without being asked whenever anyone at Iron
Bell uses the word rescued, which he has come to regard as a technical
term requiring correction rather than a kindness requiring gratitude.

NO FURTHER PARTICULARS REQUIRED

The transcription had taken him four attempts to get right, working from
memory alone since the actual muster book was an ocean and a century and a
half away and no request to view it would have made any sense to anyone
who might have received it, and he had settled, in the end, on the wording
he was fairly certain was correct — *lost overboard, Cape Finisterre, no
further particulars required* — six words that had been sufficient, in
1799, to close a man's account with His Majesty's Navy and had proved,
Ferrer had discovered, equally sufficient to open one with an American
programme a hundred and forty-four years later, the same six words doing
service, without alteration, as both an ending and a beginning, which he
had found, on first understanding it, the single most economical sentence
he had ever encountered and had disliked it more with each year he spent
considering it.

What the sentence did not require, and had never required, was his
opinion, and this was the distinction he had begun making, gently and
then less gently, to anyone at the installation who used the word rescued
in his hearing — that the muster book's no further particulars required
was doing exactly what it said, requiring nothing of him, not his consent
to be transcribed as lost, not his agreement that going overboard and
being retrieved by an American time-travel programme amounted to the same
transaction as a proper Christian burial at sea, not even his presence,
strictly, since the entry had been written by a clerk who had certainly
never met him and had filled the space the way a clerk fills any space,
with the minimum that would satisfy the ledger, and the programme, when it
came looking a century and a half later, had wanted exactly that same
minimum and had found it, and had taken him on precisely the same
authority the clerk had used to write him off, which was to say none that
had ever consulted him.

He had asked, formally, twice, to be returned, and had received, both
times, from Wexford, a refusal written in a hand that was otherwise the
plainest hand at the installation and that became, only in these two
letters, difficult to parse, the sentences turning back on themselves in a
way Ferrer recognised, having done a great deal of careful writing himself
by then, as the hand of a man avoiding a sentence he did not want to finish,
and Ferrer had filed both refusals with the muster book copy, in the same
drawer, because he had come to think of the two documents as making the
same argument from opposite ends of a century and a half — one asserting
that his consent had never been required to lose him, the other
demonstrating, by its very evasiveness, that his consent was not required
to keep him either, and that between the two of them there had never
actually been a moment, in a hundred and forty-four years, in which
anybody had thought to ask.

He did not raise it again. He did, however, continue to correct the word
rescued whenever he heard it, precisely and without heat, the way a man
corrects a measurement he has checked and is confident of, and he had
noticed, without remarking on it to anyone, that fewer people used the
word around him now than had a year ago, which he took, cautiously, to be
progress of a kind, though progress toward what he had not yet decided.
