PFC DANIEL OKONKWO

Runs the departure desk, which means he is the last name on paper before a
man goes out to Frame Three and the first face that says nothing has
changed when he comes back — a small job, mostly initials and timestamps,
that has quietly made him the only fixed point some of the settled see
twice in a day.

He signs Able Seaman Reyes-Whitlock out every single time, and has begun,
without deciding to, writing the name twice on the board. Nobody has told
him to. He has not asked himself why closely enough to get an answer he
can use.

THE SECOND LINE

The board only needs a name written once, and Okonkwo knows this the way
he knows the rest of the departure procedure, by the number of times he
has done it without a manual, and he could not, if Vosburgh asked him
directly, produce a reason that the O of REYES-WHITLOCK appears at the top
of the slot and again three lines down, in the same chalk, in a hand that
has not gotten any less steady the fortieth time than it was the first —
he only knows that the board looks wrong to him with a single line in it
now, the way a sentence looks wrong with a word left out that nobody else
would notice was missing.

He had started it in the spring, around the time he'd begun double-
checking the timestamp on Reyes-Whitlock's returns against the timestamp
on his departures, a habit that had made sense to him at the time as
ordinary thoroughness, the kind of small extra care a clerk develops for a
man who spends more hours behind the Frame than anyone else on the roster,
and it was only somewhere in early June, an evening he still could not
entirely account for, that he had gone looking for Reyes-Whitlock in the
mess hall and walked the length of the room twice before finding him at
the chair by the door, and had heard himself say I didn't see you in a
voice that surprised him with how literally it meant what it said, and had
gone back to the desk that night and, without consciously deciding to,
written the name twice on the board for the coming week's schedule, and
had left it that way ever since.

He does not think of himself as a superstitious man. He does not write any
other name twice — not Bright's, who has been at the installation nearly
as long, not his own commanding officer's, not even the Dutchman's, who
unsettles half the camp without needing to try — and when he notices this,
which he does now and then, standing at the board at the end of a shift
with the chalk still in his hand, he arrives every time at the same place,
which is that there is a reason, and that the reason is available to him
if he were willing to stand still and look at it properly, and that he has
so far always found something else that needed doing first.

Tonight the something else is Reyes-Whitlock's own signature, coming back
in at eleven-forty, on time, in a hand exactly as legible as it was going
out, and Okonkwo checks it against the departure line, and finds them
matching, and finds himself, for reasons he does not examine, checking a
second time before he closes the ledger for the night.
