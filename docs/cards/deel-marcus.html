CWO MARCUS DEEL

Runs the Line Shed the way a farmer reads weather — by ear, by smell, by the
particular give of a dial under his thumb — because nobody at Camp Iron Bell
has ever fully understood Tesla's notes and Deel is the only one honest
enough to say so out loud, in front of Wexford, more than once.

He filed the retrieval application himself. Cabinet Nine keeps the denial,
and the request stapled behind it, in his own handwriting, submitted in the
genuine expectation of an answer.

THE SOUND A BUS MAKES BEFORE IT DOESN'T

At eleven each night Deel walks the shed the long way, past Frame One and
its cold rig, past the tarp in the corner nobody has been made to move
because the paper that would let them move it has been waiting eleven
months for a man's replacement to sign for equipment the replacement never
received, and he does not look at the chalk on the floor there, because he
has looked at it enough times that looking has stopped doing anything.

He listens at Frame Three instead, ear an inch off the housing, the way he
has listened every night for six years, and what he hears tonight is the
same hum as every other night except for one half-second in the middle
third of it, a catch so small that no gauge on the panel would show it and
no manual — there being no manual, Tesla having written for himself and not
for whoever came after him — would tell him what it meant.

He had heard something like it once before, in October of 1943, a catch he
had written off as the cold, the shed being bitter that week and copper
contracting the way copper does, and he had made the adjustment his ear
told him to make, confidently, the way he had made a hundred adjustments
before it, and in November the bus he had re-routed fed Frame Two instead
of Frame Three, because Fitch's crew had swapped the load that morning
without telling him, which was not Deel's failure by any form Vosburgh had
ever managed to draft, and was entirely Deel's fault by the only ledger
that has ever mattered to Deel, which does not have a box for whose
morning it was.

He puts his palm flat on the housing now, feeling for the catch again, and
does not adjust anything, and stands there long enough that the second
shift comes on and finds him still listening, and asks him nothing, because
everyone in the Line Shed has learned not to interrupt Deel while he is
deciding whether tonight is the night his ear is wrong again.

He decides, eventually, the way he decides most nights, that it is fine,
and it usually is fine, and he goes to the drawer where he keeps a carbon
of the second application he has never sent — the one asking, this time,
not for Tesla to come back and finish the work, but only to be shown, once,
whether copper does what Deel has always told himself it does — and he
reads it standing up, and puts it back, and returns to Frame Three to
listen through the shift's end.
