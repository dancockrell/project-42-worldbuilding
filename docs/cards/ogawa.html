KEEPER OGAWA

Sixty-eight, has tended Light 7's lamp since 1927, was never transferred,
holds no clearance of any kind — the programme decided removing him would
require explaining him to the Maritime Bureau, and explaining him was more
trouble than leaving him. Forty minutes every night to trim and light it,
for ships that mostly no longer sail past. He has watched four hundred
personnel come and go and been briefed on none of it. Several have told
him things they have told nobody else at the station, because he is the
only person there not required to write anything down, and does not know
enough to be dangerous with any of it.

THE ELEVEN-SECOND SWEEP

The light took forty minutes to trim and light properly, the way it had
taken forty minutes every night since 1927, and Ogawa had long ago stopped
noticing the climb the way a man stops noticing his own knees on a
staircase he has used ten thousand times, and was noticing, instead, as he
had for three years now, the particular quality of a station that runs on
the assumption that an old man in a keeper's coat cannot be listening,
because he is not the kind of person the paperwork was built to account
for.

The corporal came up the tower stair a little after one, which was not
against any rule Ogawa knew of, because there was no rule against a man
who could not sleep going somewhere the light was, and stood at the rail
for a while without saying anything, the way most of them did at first,
watching the beam go out across water that mostly no longer carried the
ships it was meant for, and Ogawa made the tea he always made, in the pot
he had used since before the programme existed, and set it down within
reach and asked nothing, because asking was the one skill every young
officer at the station seemed to have and he had made a quiet, private
policy, thirty years deep now, of being the one man there who did not.

It came out sideways, the way it always did — not a confession, exactly,
more a fact set down in the wrong room by a man who had been carrying it
through rooms where it did not fit. The corporal said the light on the
barracks ceiling every eleven seconds had stopped waking him months ago,
which should have been a relief and was not, because he had realized, lying
there counting a sweep he could no longer feel, that he could not remember
the last time anything at the station had woken him at all, and that this
frightened him more than the light ever had, and he did not know who at
the station he could say that to without it going into a file, and Ogawa
was the only person he could think of who did not keep one.

Ogawa said that the light had been sweeping that ceiling since before the
barracks existed to have a ceiling, and that he had never once been asked
to change the timing, and that he supposed some things simply went on
doing what they had always done regardless of who had stopped feeling
them, and the corporal took this the way most of them took whatever Ogawa
offered — as more comfort than the words alone should have carried — and
went back down the tower a little after two, and Ogawa finished the tea
alone, and logged the weather, and said nothing to anyone in the morning,
because there was, in the whole thirteen hundred pages the programme had
generated about that station, no form with a line for it.
