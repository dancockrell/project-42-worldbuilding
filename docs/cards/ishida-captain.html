CAPTAIN ISHIDA

Liaison officer, and the only person at Light Seven who has read the
treaty as though it were a promise made to him rather than a document
Amatsu wrote to survive an audit. He has drafted a clarification under
Article VI and is waiting for a reply. He will be waiting for the whole
war.

Sincere, decent, entirely wrong, and correct about every sentence he has
actually read — which is the trouble, since he has never once turned four
pages further, to the one that says the document was never signed.

ARTICLE VI

He had explained it to Mifune the way he explained it to anyone new enough
to need it explained, unfolding the treaty's sixth article the way another
man might unfold a family document at a bedside — the First Party
undertakes to provide for the welfare, the medical attention, the proper
treatment of selected persons, and affirms that selected persons are
soldiers of the Empire and entitled to be so regarded — and Mifune had
listened the way young men listen to something that sounds like an
insurance policy, which was, Ishida privately felt, exactly what it was,
and a good one, drafted by someone who had clearly taken the trouble to
get the language right rather than merely official.

He was not a stupid man and had never let himself be told he was one. He
had read Article VI more times than anyone alive, checked its clauses
against two others he had found in a captured Chinese file for comparison,
and satisfied himself, over the better part of a year, that the drafting
was sound — every obligation stated plainly, every entitlement named,
nothing hedged, nothing softened into a phrase a lawyer could later argue
away — and it had not once occurred to him, in that year of careful
reading, to ask who the obligation actually bound, because the answer felt
so obviously outward that the question did not present itself as a
question at all. The First Party undertakes. He read undertakes as a
promise extended to him. It was, in fact, a promise the Empire had made to
itself, about itself, enforceable by no one, and he had built a year of
patience on top of a sentence whose grammar he had understood perfectly
and whose subject he had never once checked.

The clarification he had drafted and sent through channels asked only
whether Article VI's welfare provisions extended to a man's dependants as
well as to the selected themselves, a reasonable question, competently
argued, and he had been waiting eleven months for an answer, which he
attributed to wartime channels being wartime channels, and which was in
fact unanswerable, because there was no office anywhere that held the
other half of the correspondence, because no other half existed, because
nothing had ever signed for it.

He kept his own copy of the treaty in the second drawer of his desk, the
later pages still uncut from binding — there had never been occasion to
turn past Article VI, everything he needed being there — and some evenings,
waiting on the clarification, he took it out and read the sixth article
again, aloud sometimes, to no one, the way a man rereads a letter from
home not because the words have changed but because rereading them is its
own kind of company, and he found, every time, that it still said exactly
what he remembered it saying, which was true, and which he took, each
time, as further reason to trust it.
