MRS IDA PRENTISS

Holds no rank the Army recognises as authority and holds, in practice, the
installation itself, in the specific sense that every form generated by
Camp Iron Bell passes across her desk before it reaches anyone with the
power to act on it, and she has never once, in three years, been asked
how she decides what goes up first.

She has correctly predicted the outcome of eleven retrieval authorisations
from the paperwork alone, a fact she does not know is remarkable, having
never been told it, and would very likely find the compliment insulting if
anyone thought to offer it, on the grounds that predicting the outcome of
a form is simply what reading the form correctly produces.

THE THREE TRAYS

Nobody had labelled the trays, and Prentiss had never labelled them
herself, on the grounds that a label was a decision written down where
someone else could read it and disagree with, and she preferred her
decisions the way she preferred most things at Camp Iron Bell, correct and
unexplained, arrived at through a process that produced, consistently, the
right order for the day's business to reach Wexford's desk without her
ever having to defend, to Wexford or to anyone above him, why the milk
cooler complaint had gone up ahead of the requisition for surgical
dressings, or why a particular 42-A had sat in the middle tray for two
days while she waited for something she could not have named to resolve
itself before sending it forward.

She had come to the installation in 1943 as a typist, hired locally, with
no clearance and no expectation of one, and had discovered, within her
first month, that the work of an installation this size was not, in
practice, the work described on the organisation chart, which put Wexford
at the top and everyone else in a tidy descending order beneath him, but
was instead the work of deciding, several dozen times a day, which piece
of paper mattered more than which other piece of paper, a decision the
chart did not assign to anyone and that she had simply begun making,
quietly, because someone had to and no one else appeared to have noticed
that the making of it was itself a kind of authority, one that did not
require a signature, a rank, or a clearance, only a desk positioned
between everyone who wrote things down and everyone who acted on what was
written.

She took her leave in July, two weeks, every year, and had noticed,
without ever being told, that Major Vosburgh generally arranged to be at
Hazlehurst for the duration, a coincidence she had chosen, for three
years running, not to remark on, partly because remarking on it would
have required acknowledging what the coincidence meant, and partly
because she found, on reflection, that she did not want the arrangement
examined closely enough that someone might decide to fix it, there being,
in her experience, no faster way to lose a good arrangement than to make
someone responsible for it look directly at what it was.

She had never asked for a clearance, in three years, and had come to
understand that this was the actual foundation of whatever authority she
had, rather than an oversight in it — that a woman who wanted access to
the installation's secrets would eventually be given some and denied
others, and would spend her days negotiating the boundary between the two,
whereas a woman who wanted nothing beyond the ordinary business of sorting
paper was trusted with all of it, without exception, on the theory that
nothing she saw could compromise anything, a theory she had done nothing,
in three years, to either confirm or dispel.
