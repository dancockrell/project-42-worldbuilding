Hyakki Yakō · Unit · B tier

TENGU-SCOUT

Chosen while carrying water, according to the only account of it that
exists, and given sight that reaches past what the eye should manage —
not flight, whatever the folklore's name for him implies; the folklore is
not a manual and never has been. He files reports on ground nobody else
has walked, and every one of them has been correct, which the programme
has come to rely on and has never once managed to trust, because
correctness with nothing to check it against is not the same thing as
being believed.

THE VALLEY NOBODY HAD MAPPED

He had been sent ahead of the column the way he was always sent ahead, on
the strength of a request for reconnaissance that assumed a man would
climb the ridge, look, and come back uncertain the way scouts always came
back — with a probable troop strength, a likely position, the honest
hedging of somebody reporting what he had glimpsed rather than what he
knew — and he had come back instead, as he always did, with numbers, flat
and exact, delivered in the same voice he used to ask for his rations,
and the lieutenant taking the report had written it down without argument,
because arguing with a Tengu-Scout's numbers had stopped, eleven months
in, being something anyone at the column bothered to do.

What none of them asked, because asking would have required a vocabulary
none of them owned, was how a man crouched on a ridge at four hundred
meters could report the precise count of a supply dump under canvas in a
valley he had never walked into, and he could not have answered them
plainly if they had, because the seeing did not arrive to him as a
picture he could describe standing over — it arrived the way a fact
arrives to a man who has always known it, sourceless and complete, so that
by the time he opened his eyes on the ridge the valley was simply
furnished for him, counted and certain, in the same manner a room is
furnished before you enter it rather than while you look around.

He had gone, in the first months, back over his own reports afterward,
checking them the honest way a soldier checks anything, comparing what he
had said against what the column found when it arrived, in the private
hope of finding himself wrong even once, because a scout who is
occasionally wrong is a scout doing a soldier's job, and a scout who is
never wrong is doing something he has no honest word for and no comfort
in. He had found himself wrong zero times in eleven months, and had
stopped checking sometime in the ninth, not from confidence but because
each confirmation had begun to cost him something the wrongness would
have given back.

He crouched now on a ridge above ground the maps did not carry, watching
a valley furnish itself in front of him the way it always did, complete
and correct and entirely without the honest labor of having looked, and
felt, as he felt every time, the small cold certainty of a man watching
his own hand write a report he has not yet decided to make — and wrote it
anyway, because the column was two hours behind him and would need the
numbers, and the numbers, once again, would be right.
