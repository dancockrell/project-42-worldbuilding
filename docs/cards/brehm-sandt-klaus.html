OBERREGIERUNGSRAT KLAUS BREHM-SANDT

Director, Hollernbruch. A civil servant, not a soldier and not a
scientist, and by every measure anyone has ever applied to him — a
colleague, a neighbour, his daughters — a decent man. He signs
everything. He is punctual. He is afraid of Section Six, which is
correct, and he would never say so aloud.

He does not choose what happens at Hollernbruch. He makes it possible by
never once asking to see it, and the not-asking is the whole of his
contribution, and it is enough.

THE VISIT

Brehm-Sandt's elder daughter came up from Breslau for the weekend in
July, seventeen and serious about it, wanting to see where her father
worked before she went back to school and had to describe it to people
who asked, and he had said yes before he had thought it through,
because saying no to her about anything had never once occurred to him
as a thing a father did.

He gave her the tour a director gives a daughter: the corridor with its
tile still washable after fifty years, the theatre list on the old
visiting-hours board that nobody had ever thought to take down, the
solarium with its grey paint and the green light coming through it in
the afternoon in a way she said was beautiful, and he agreed that it
was, because it was, and neither of them found anything strange in
saying so standing where they stood.

He did not take her to the kennels, because the smell in July was bad
and there was no reason to explain it to her, and he did not take her
past the north stair, because the stair was Procedure access only and
the sign said so and a director does not need a better reason than the
sign. He did not take her to Kadaver, because the theatres were working
that afternoon and a working theatre was not a place for visitors, and
this was, strictly, true, and he told himself so with the same
unhurried confidence with which he told her the solarium was beautiful.

At dinner in the officers' mess she asked him, brightly, what agricultural
research a man actually did all day, having read the word on the sign at
the gate and taken it, reasonably, as the whole of the answer, and he
told her about yields and estimates and the trouble with silage in a
wet year, and she nodded and ate her soup and believed him completely,
because there was nothing in his voice for her to disbelieve — he was
not performing calm, he simply was calm, having spent thirteen years
arranging his own days so that no part of them required him to know
anything he would have had to lie about to tell her.

She left on the Sunday train, delighted with the visit, and wrote to her
mother that Papa's work was terribly dull and the marsh smelled
dreadful and the light in that glass room was the loveliest thing she
had ever seen, and Brehm-Sandt read the letter over his wife's shoulder
weeks later and agreed with every word of it, and signed four
requisitions before lunch, and went home that second weekend exactly on
schedule, as he always did.
