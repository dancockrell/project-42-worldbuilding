# PROJECT 42 — COLONEL NORMAN WEXFORD

**Fork A.** The man who holds position 1 and means it completely.

He has been cited in eight files and has never had one. The Bright Memorandum
makes that a gap rather than an omission, because the memorandum's whole power is
that it is addressed to him and he has not read it.

---

## 1. Played straight, and that is a craft instruction

**Wexford is not a villain, not a fool, and not secretly cynical.** He is a
competent regular officer running an impossible installation, and he is written
dead straight so that the comedy everywhere else in the faction has something to
land against.

If a scene makes Wexford absurd, the scene is wrong. **He is the only person at
Camp Iron Bell who is never funny.**

---

## 2. What he believes

> *"Every person here was going to die, or vanish, or be forgotten. We are the
> only reason any of them still exist. Name the harm."*

He means it, and the strongest version of it is not sentimental: a retrieval only
closes if the record already lost the subject, so **by construction every settled
person was already gone.** He is not taking futures from people. He is taking
people out of a hole the century had already dug.

**He also believes the programme is steering history.**

It is not. The Consistency Finding says the loop is already closed and everything
Camp Iron Bell has ever done was always already done. **Wexford has read the
Finding. He signs off on Paradox Audit reports that confirm it. He has not
connected the two things**, and nobody has forced him to, and the game never
forces him either.

**This is the faction's central irony and it is never stated to him or to the
player.** §00.

---

## 3. Nineteen

Bright's memorandum argues, correctly, that the limit on retrievals is money.

**Wexford has never once thought of it as a budget.**

The programme is nineteen people because nineteen people is the number he can
protect — the number whose names he knows, whose billets he has walked past,
whose files he has signed personally. He would be appalled to see it written as
an accounting outcome, and he would not be able to answer the arithmetic.

**Bright is right about the numbers and wrong about him.** Both halves of that
are load-bearing and neither cancels the other.

---

## 4. What he actually does all day

Not physics. **Signatures.**

Three per retrieval on W.D. 42-A, and he reads every one. He has declined four
authorisations in eighteen months, all on grounds of subject condition rather
than cost, and each refusal is longer than the approval it replaced.

He walks the Annex on Thursdays. He does not join the card game and has never
been invited, and he arranges his walk so that he passes while it is happening.

He has written nineteen letters — one per settled person — to be opened in the
event of his death, and Prentiss knows they exist because she typed the envelopes
and has never asked what is in them.

---

## 5. The two things he will not do

**He will not send anyone back.** Retrieval is one-way as physics; keeping them
at Iron Bell afterward is a policy, and it is his. He has been asked twice — once
by Ferrer, formally, in writing — and refused both times, and the refusals are the
only documents in the camp where his prose becomes evasive.

**He will not discuss the county.** The two fountains outside Building 1, the
billeting denials, the ordinance citations. He signs what the state requires and
he does not defend it, explain it, or apologise for it, and he has never once
been heard to say anything about it in any direction.

**That silence is the most damning thing in his file** and Volume III already
records the programme's own version of it: it has not found the contradiction
interesting enough to write down anywhere.

---

## 6. Playing him

- **Never write him as steering.** Write him as *believing he is steering*, which
  is a different performance and a sadder one.
- **He is generous, personally.** He learns names, he remembers illnesses, he is
  good at the part of command that is about people. That is precisely why
  nineteen feels to him like a family and not a figure.
- **He never explains the Finding.** He can. He has read it. He does not connect
  it to himself, and a scene that has him do so is the wrong scene.
- **Do not have him read the memorandum.** Ever. Prime has flagged this and it is
  right: the memorandum's power is entirely that it is unsent.

---

## 7. Open, deliberately

- What is in the nineteen letters.
- Whether he has ever suspected the Finding applies to him.
- **Whether he has ever counted how many of the nineteen were not already
  lost.** Three were not. Bright, Cheung and Kell are forward-settled, and
  the record did not lose them — it has not reached them yet. His defence in
  §2 covers sixteen people and he has never noticed which three it does not.
  *This is deliberate. Do not qualify the claim in §2 to fix it, and never
  let him work it out.* It is the same blindness as the Finding, with three
  names attached who eat in his mess, and it is the one argument Bright could
  make that would actually reach him — which she cannot, because the
  memorandum stays unsent.
- Why he times the Thursday walk.
