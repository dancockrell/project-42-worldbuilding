# PROJECT 42 — VOLUME III: CAMP IRON BELL

**The place all three games touch.** World Aflame's Allied faction is billeted
here. The Long Night's orientation and tutorial happen here. Ghost Front's agent
deploys from here and comes back to it.

So it gets built properly, once, in detail, and everyone uses the same one.
Where this document and a game's local invention disagree, this wins — bring the
disagreement to the lore thread rather than forking the camp.

Nothing here is a real place, unit, or person. The Mississippi, the segregation,
the year and the war are real. Everything inside the wire is ours.

---

## 1. What it is, and what it says it is

**Camp Iron Bell (Signal Corps Training Annex)**, Copiah County, Mississippi.
Established March 1943 on the site of a failed pecan concern, forty minutes from
anywhere on a road the county has twice declined to pave.

The cover is radio school. It is a *good* cover, because it explains the
antennas, the fencing, the men who come and go at odd hours, and — partly — the
electricity. The locals believe it and find it dull, which is the entire
objective. A place nobody asks about is worth more than a place nobody can find.

The name means nothing. It was drawn from a list. **The programme's own people
assume it is an acronym and there is a standing bet, now four hundred dollars,
on what for.** Nobody at the War Department can settle it because the list was
generated to be meaningless, which is the only genuinely funny thing the
Department has ever done on purpose.

### The power problem, which is the cover's one soft spot

Iron Bell draws current on a scale no signals school could justify. On a
retrieval night the lights brown out in three towns and the substation at
Hazlehurst makes a noise people describe, unprompted and identically, as *a
swallow*.

The cover story for that is a canning plant. There is no canning plant.

And the honest, load-bearing, entirely mundane consequence, which is the shape of
this whole world: a dairy farmer three miles east has written **four letters**
about his milk cooler. Not about the light in the sky. Not about the noise. About
the cooler, which cuts out, and the milk, which spoils, and the eleven dollars.

Two of those letters are in the file. Two are not, and nobody at Iron Bell knows
where they went, and that is the sort of thing that keeps Major Vosburgh awake
rather than the physics.

## 2. The hum

**Everything at Iron Bell hums at sixty cycles.** The wire, the huts, the
bedframes, the fillings in your teeth. Newcomers cannot sleep for a week.
Everyone else stopped hearing it in the second month and now cannot hear its
*absence* either, which is worse, because the hum dropping out means a frame has
taken the load and something is about to happen or has just failed.

Personnel who have been here longer than a year have a particular tinnitus. The
medical officer has a name for it that is not in any manual.

**The mess hall is the only building on the site that is acoustically dead** — it
was built into the old pecan drying shed, thick brick, no conduit through the
walls. People eat there when they are not hungry. Men sit at empty tables at
eleven at night, not talking, for the quiet. Nobody has ever said out loud that
this is what they are doing. The cooks know and put the urn on.

---

## 3. The site

- **The Wire.** Two fences, a gravel road between them, no dogs. Dogs will not
  settle here and two attempts were abandoned; the file records the reason as
  *unsuitable temperament*, which is not what the handler wrote.
- **Building 1 — Administration.** Where the forms live. See §5.
- **Building 3 — the Frame Hall.** The reason for the camp. See §4. There is no
  Building 2. The numbering skips it and the skip is not documented, which has
  generated more speculation than anything else on the site.
- **The Annex.** Married quarters that no married person occupies. Where the
  settled are billeted. See §6.
- **The Line Shed.** Tesla plant. Oil-cooled, copper, and the only building with
  a fire watch posted around the clock. Smells of hot varnish and ozone. The men
  who work it are half-deaf and unusually calm.
- **Mess.** See §2.
- **The Ballfield.** Scraped flat in 1943, never used for baseball. It is the
  only open ground large enough to lay something out on, and everyone knows what
  that means, and it is still called the ballfield.

## 4. The Frame Hall

Four frames. Copper and oil and glass, each the size of a barn door standing on
end, each fed by its own bus from the Line Shed.

- **Frame One** is a teaching rig. It has never moved anything living and is used
  to show new personnel what the noise sounds like from inside the building.
- **Frame Two** killed Sergeant Aurelio Fitch in November 1943. It has not been
  dismantled, because dismantling requires a disposition form countersigned by an
  officer who has since been reassigned, and the replacement will not sign for
  equipment he did not receive. **It stands in the corner under a tarp with a
  chalk mark on the floor around it that nobody re-draws and nobody scuffs out.**
- **Frame Three** works. Everything the programme has ever recovered came through
  Frame Three.
- **Frame Four** is Frame Three's spare, built from the same drawings, and has
  never once closed a retrieval. The engineers have measured everything and the
  two frames are identical to the limits of what they can measure. Nobody says
  the obvious thing about Frame Three being *the one that already worked*,
  because saying it out loud sounds like superstition and thinking it quietly
  sounds like physics.

### What a retrieval is actually like

Not a flash. **A retrieval takes between nine and forty minutes**, and it is
mostly waiting, and the waiting is the part nobody warns you about.

The hum drops. The oil note in the Line Shed climbs a fifth. Then the air in the
hall goes *thick* — men describe it as swimming, or as being spoken to in a
language they nearly know — and the arrival is not sudden but gradual and
sideways, like something becoming true rather than appearing.

The subject arrives on the floor, always on the floor, always facing away from
Frame Three. Nobody has explained the facing. It is in every log and no report
has ever remarked on it.

**Then they are sick.** Every time, without exception, and the medical detail
comes in with the buckets before the retrieval starts, because they know.

The programme's single most tightly held operational fact is that this is
undignified. A man is pulled out of the wreck of his own century by the greatest
scientific apparatus ever assembled and the first thing that happens to him is he
vomits on a concrete floor while a corporal holds his head. There is no version
of the paperwork that contains this and everyone in the hall has seen it.

## 5. The forms

The programme's real nervous system, and its voice.

| Form | What it is |
|---|---|
| **W.D. 42-A** | Retrieval authorisation. Three signatures, one of them Wexford's. |
| **W.D. 42-B** | *Does the subject exist prior to issue?* The question is about kit, not metaphysics — you cannot issue boots to a man with no service number — and it has ruined more than one clerk who thought about it too long. |
| **W.D. 42-C** | Condition and coherence. The one an agent sees. It understates, and it understates worst when things are worst. |
| **W.D. 42-D** | **Consistency certification.** *"Is the subject's absence consistent with the surviving record?"* One box. One set of initials. This is the form the whole programme actually turns on and it fits on half a page. |
| **W.D. 42-R** | Denial. |

### Cabinet Nine

The 42-Rs live in Cabinet Nine in Building 1, bottom drawer, and there are more
of them than of everything else combined.

**"Filed in nine"** is camp slang for anything that is not going to happen. Leave
requests are filed in nine. A better generator is filed in nine. Somebody's
transfer to a posting with weather is filed in nine.

And the Tesla denial is in there — the one that came back on a man the programme
needed more than anyone who has ever lived, because his death was too well
attested to work around. People know which drawer. Occasionally somebody new is
shown it, and the showing is a small ceremony nobody would admit to performing.

### Mrs. Ida Prentiss

Civilian clerk, forty-one, hired locally in 1943 to type, and now the person
through whom every form on the base passes.

She has no clearance for what the programme does and has never asked. She has
correctly predicted the outcome of eleven retrieval authorisations from the
paperwork alone, which she does not know is remarkable because nobody has told
her, and she would find the compliment insulting.

**She is, functionally, the most powerful person at Camp Iron Bell.** Wexford
signs; Ida decides what reaches him and in what order, and she has never once
been wrong about which two things must not be on the same desk on the same
morning. When she takes her fortnight in July the camp does not run and the
programme's response has been to stagger the July retrievals rather than to
examine why.

## 6. The settled

**"Settled"** is the programme's word for a retrieved person. Retrieval is
one-way. The word was chosen by somebody in Washington who has never been here.
The settled use it too, with an inflection.

They are billeted in the Annex, which was built as married quarters, which means
it has curtains and a kitchen and a small square of grass, and is therefore the
only part of Iron Bell that resembles a life rather than a posting. This was not
kindness. It was a housing shortage. It has had the effect of kindness anyway and
nobody has corrected it.

### What they are actually like

Not oracles. **Tired, and displaced, and frequently bored.**

The ones from the past find the plumbing miraculous and the war depressingly
familiar. The ones from the future are worse company, because they have the
manners of a century that has not happened and because they keep almost saying
things. There is an etiquette, unwritten and absolutely enforced, that **you do
not ask a forward-settled how it comes out.** Two men who did are no longer at
Iron Bell and their files say *reassigned*.

The settled hold a card game in the Annex on Thursdays that has accreted rules
from four centuries and is no longer playable by anyone who was not there when a
given rule was added. They will teach it to you. You will not understand it. This
is, everyone agrees, entirely the point.

### The county, which is the bill

Camp Iron Bell sits in Mississippi in 1944 and the Army it belongs to is
segregated. The programme's own regulations do not care where anyone is from —
they care what a person can do — and so, inside the wire, the programme is more
egalitarian than the country it is defending. Outside the wire, in Copiah County,
none of that obtains.

**The programme has never resolved this and has never tried.** It has instead
produced a body of local accommodations: which men go into town and which do not,
whose billet is where, which two fountains stand outside Building 1 and what is
painted above them. All of it documented, in the same flat voice as the
requisitions, filed and forgotten.

A forward-settled engineer arriving from a later century walks past those two
fountains on her first morning and asks what they are for.

**The asking is the problem.** Not the answer — everyone knows the answer. What
Iron Bell cannot metabolise is somebody standing in front of an arrangement
everyone has agreed not to see and *requiring it to be explained out loud.* The
file records this as a disciplinary matter. It does not record what she said,
because the clerk who took the report could not make it fit the form.

## 7. Personnel

All invented. Use them, add to them, do not contradict them.

- **Colonel Norman Wexford**, commanding. Played dead straight so the comedy
  elsewhere lands. Believes the programme is steering history. He is wrong (the
  Consistency Finding) and the fact that he is wrong is not something the game
  ever tells him or the player directly.
- **Major Elias Vosburgh**, executive officer. Runs the camp in practice. Is kept
  awake by the milk cooler, not the physics, and this is the correct thing to be
  kept awake by.
- **Captain (Dr) Naomi Falk**, medical officer. Signs the coherence codes on
  W.D. 42-C. **Stopped believing the bands eight months ago** and signs them
  anyway, because the alternative is not signing them, and she has worked out
  what that would cost and who would pay it. She is the closest thing the
  programme has to a conscience and she is fully complicit, which is the point.
- **Chief Warrant Officer Marcus Deel**, Line Shed. Half-deaf, unhurried, the
  only man who will say plainly that nobody understands the Tesla notes and that
  they are running the apparatus by ear.
- **Mrs Ida Prentiss**, civilian clerk. §5.
- **Sergeant Aurelio Fitch**, deceased, Frame Two, November 1943. Present in the
  camp as a chalk outline nobody redraws, a form nobody will sign, and a name
  people avoid using in the Frame Hall while using it freely in the mess.
- **Staff Sergeant Ada Moreau**, Current. Camp-native, not settled. Stands in the
  gap between a programme whose paperwork is more egalitarian than the state it
  sits in, and the state. Playable protagonist, The Long Night.
- **Corporal Silas Hune**, Phase. Camp-native, born 1944 — not one of the
  Settled, which is the point: he was never taken, he simply used the pillar too
  often on his own initiative, and the medical files use the same word,
  *attenuation*, for a man who did it to himself as for someone the programme
  did it to. A quieter case than Reyes-Whitlock and a useful contrast to him.
  Playable protagonist, The Long Night.
- **Warrant Officer Emmet Doig**, Current, quartermaster. Signs the requisitions
  and has never once asked what they are for. Complicity as texture, in Deel's
  shed rather than Falk's office. Playable protagonist, The Long Night.

## 8. Register — how to write this place

- **Nobody is amazed.** Magical realism (§0c). The marvellous is reported flatly.
  A man arrives from 1806 and the sentence that matters is the one about the
  bucket.
- **The comedy is institutional.** Forms, denials, cost, procedure, the four
  hundred dollar bet. Never on what happens to people (rule 10).
- **The horror is adjacent and quiet.** A tarp. A chalk line. A drawer. A man
  eating alone in the only room without a hum.
- **The paperwork lies and the world does not.** Whatever a form says, the thing
  it describes is available to anyone who looks.
- **Ask, do not conclude** (§00). Iron Bell never explains itself, and the
  strongest material here — the fountains, the facing-away, Frame Four, the two
  missing letters — is strong precisely because nothing resolves it.
