# PROJECT 42 — VOLUME XI: 1888

The Victorian era, for The Long Night and any other game that reaches back.

**The Consistency Finding governs everything here** and it makes 1888 the most
morally loaded destination in the setting. Read Volume III §5 (the forms) and
LORE-BIBLE.md §5 (the Finding) before writing a scene.

---

## 1. Why 1888, and why it is uncomfortable

**The programme can only take people the record already loses.**

In Camp Iron Bell that is a filing rule. In Whitechapel in 1888 it is a
description of a class of person, and the programme's criteria and the era's
indifference **turn out to select for the same people.**

Who does the record lose in London in 1888?

The casual poor. Women in the workhouse infirmary. Dock labourers hired by the
half-day. Children in the rookeries with no registered birth. People who moved
between lodging houses under names they chose that week. The unnamed dead in the
Thames. **Anyone the census had no method for and the parish had no reason to
follow.**

Every one of them passes W.D. 42-D on the first reading.

**So a retrieval team standing in Whitechapel is standing in the richest
harvesting ground the programme has ever found, and the reason it is rich is
that Victorian London did not count these people.** The Finding is physics. What
it selects for here is not.

**The programme has never written this down.** Volume VII records the same
observation about the roster and says it has never been made; this is where it
becomes impossible to miss and still nobody makes it. That is the point. Do not
have a character deliver it as a speech. Let a player stand in the room and
arrive at it.

### The bridge already exists

**Dr Miriam Halloran-Sze came from here** — a workhouse infirmary doctor in
Whitechapel whose entire caseload was people nobody counted, who vanished from
the record in 1889 and whom nobody looked for (Volume VII).

She is the cleanest possible illustration and she is already canon: **the
programme took the doctor because the doctor was as uncounted as her patients.**

## 2. What a retrieval into 1888 is like

Frame Three reaches. It does not send a building — it sends people, at ruinous
cost, and they arrive the way anything arrives: gradually, sideways, on the
floor, facing away from nothing at all because there is no Frame at this end.

**And then they are sick**, same as always, in an alley, in the dark, while
somebody holds their head. The undignified fact travels.

Practical constraints for any scene:

- **A team is two or three people.** Mass costs energy.
- **They bring nothing anachronistic they cannot account for.** Not from
  caution — from the Finding. An object that history would have noticed is an
  object that cannot be there.
- **The window is not generous.** Iron Bell schedules against the Line Shed's
  capacity, not against the mission's convenience, and a team that misses its
  window waits for the next one.
- **Nobody at this end knows anything.** No allies, no safe house, no
  contingency. 1888 is not aware of them and will not help.

## 3. Goulston Street — the tutorial, and the ruling already made

**30 September 1888.** A piece of apron cut from **Catherine Eddowes**, murdered
hours earlier in Mitre Square, found in a stairwell doorway in Goulston Street.
Chalked writing on the wall above it, transcribed differently by the men who saw
it and never reconciled.

**City of London officers wanted it photographed** — the apron was theirs,
Eddowes having been killed on City ground. **Superintendent Thomas Arnold of the
Metropolitan Police wanted it erased**, because the street ran into a Jewish
quarter, the market would open at daylight, and he judged a line of writing about
Jews found beside a murdered woman's clothing would set off attacks on the people
living there. **Sir Charles Warren arrived a little after five.** The wall was
Metropolitan ground. He sided with Arnold. It was sponged off before it was
light.

There is no photograph. No handwriting was ever examined.

**Standing rulings, unchanged:**

1. **Name Catherine Eddowes.** Not "a murdered woman." Under §0a she gets her
   name, and this is the smallest possible instance of the whole philosophy.
2. **Be honest about why it was erased.** Arnold's reason was real. Both things
   are true at once and neither cancels: the erasure had a defensible motive and
   it destroyed the only copy. **Do not flatten it into "erasure bad"** — a
   lesson that all recording is virtuous is a worse and more fragile idea, and
   teaching that erasure is sometimes done for real reasons *and is still a loss*
   inoculates the player against the naive version.
3. **No Ripper mythology.** The story is Warren, Arnold, the City officers and
   the dawn deadline. It is not about the murderer, and the sensationalised
   version treats real murdered women as scenery.

**Verify ranks and the jurisdictional detail against a source before shipping.**
This volume is written from knowledge and the sourcing standard applies to me
first.

## 4. The other two factions in 1888 — a ruling

The Long Night proposed meeting retrieval teams from Werk Nachtigall and Hyakki
Yakō in the Victorian period. **That collides with canon and the fix is better
than the original.**

**Only Project 42 can do this.** Chrono is the thing the other two cannot have,
and it is half of why Werk Nachtigall is the way it is (Volume IV §3). Giving
them retrieval teams would flatten the setting's central asymmetry.

So:

### Werk Nachtigall: not a team. Debris.

**They have tried.** Of course they have — the Office does not accept that
something is unreachable, it concludes the examination was insufficient. An
Imperial attempt at the geometry, underfunded, filed under something else, and
**it did not work.**

What is in 1888 is **what a failed attempt left behind**: apparatus that should
not exist, in a cellar, cold and long dead. Material. A body, perhaps, that the
record of 1888 quietly absorbed because 1888 absorbed a great many bodies.

**No German characters. No confrontation.** A player finds equipment and works
out what it was for, and the horror is that somebody tried this and failed and
nobody came back for them.

*This is also, quietly, the darkest joke available: a Werk Nachtigall casualty in
1888 passes W.D. 42-D perfectly. Project 42 could retrieve them. Nobody has
suggested it.*

### Hyakki Yakō: not a team. It is already there.

**The watcher is not bound by time the way people are.** It selected four people
in 1941 in a building on the Noto peninsula, and it holds KNOWLEDGE 1.0 of the
March 1943 subject continuously and at any distance (Volume X, hub edges).

So there is no Japanese presence in 1888 and there does not need to be. **The
watching is.** An agent in Whitechapel who is fused, or who is near someone who
is, feels exactly what they always feel — and the correct reading is not that it
followed them, but that *it was already here and the word "already" does not
mean what they think it means.*

**Never confirm this.** No character explains it. It is felt and not stated.

## 5. Texture — what 1888 is like to stand in

Not fog and gaslight. Specifics.

- **The smell.** Coal smoke over horse dung over the river. The Thames in
  autumn. A city of four million running on animals.
- **The sound.** Iron tyres on granite setts, constantly. Street sellers. And at
  night, in the rookeries, an unnerving amount of *nothing* — the poor were
  early to bed because candles cost money.
- **Light.** Gas where it is paid for and nowhere else. The dark in a court off
  a main road in 1888 is a genuine dark that a person from 1944, let alone 2031,
  has never experienced.
- **Time.** Nobody has a watch. Bells and factory whistles. A retrieval window
  measured in minutes is being kept by people with no way to measure minutes.
- **The workhouse infirmary.** Where Halloran-Sze worked. Free, feared, and the
  only medicine most of these people will ever see.

### Period reading, and it is live rather than decorative

Long Night's thread of period authors and philosophers is welcome, and 1888
supplies an extraordinary amount:

- The year is inside the great age of the **social investigator** — people
  walking these streets with notebooks, trying to count the uncounted. A
  character doing that work is standing in direct opposition to the programme's
  entire method without knowing the programme exists.
- **That is the best dramatic irony available in this era.** Someone whose life's
  work is making the poor legible, in a place where the programme can only take
  people who are not.

**Use that.** A retrieval team's ideal subject is precisely the person a social
investigator is trying to put in the record, and if the investigator succeeds,
the subject becomes unreachable.

**The programme has an interest in these people remaining uncounted.** Nobody at
Iron Bell has said so. Nobody would. But it is true, and a player who works it
out has found the sharpest thing in the setting.
