# PROJECT 42 — VOLUME XV: LIGHT SEVEN

Hyakki Yakō's station, built to the depth of Camp Iron Bell and Hollernbruch.
Companion to Volume V, which is premise; this is the place.

**Read the guard in LORE-BIBLE.md §5 first.** These are people. The failure mode
here is not a continuity error.

---

## 1. The place has no name

The programme never named it.

It is a lighthouse and signal station on the Noto peninsula, built 1903,
staffed by the Navy until 1941 and by the programme since. The buildings are a
light, a keeper's house, a fog signal, a radio hut, and four barracks the
programme put up in six weeks and did not finish.

Every document calls it by its prewar navigation designation: **Light 7.**

Naming it would have required deciding what it was, and deciding what it was
would have required somebody to write down what happened here in August 1941.
**So it stayed a number, and thirteen hundred pages of programme correspondence
refer to the most significant site in the Empire as an entry in a mariner's
list.**

That is not an oversight. Amatsu noticed within a month and let it stand.

### The light still works

This is the detail that matters most about the place.

**Light 7 is a working lighthouse.** Its keeper, Ogawa, is sixty-eight, has
tended it since 1927, was never transferred, and has no clearance of any kind.
The programme decided it was easier to leave him than to explain his removal to
the Maritime Bureau.

So every night, in the middle of the most classified installation in Japan, an
old man climbs the tower and lights a lamp for ships that are mostly no longer
sailing, and comes down, and makes tea.

**He has watched four hundred personnel come and go and has never once
been briefed.** He is unfailingly polite to all of them. Several have told him
things they have not told the programme, because he is the only person at Light
7 who is not required to write anything down.

*He is the best character on the station and he does not know anything.*

## 2. August 1941, as the record actually has it

Two accounts exist and the programme prefers the second.

**The first-hand notes**, written that night, in three hands, disagreeing on
details and agreeing on the sequence: eleven people were in the signal hut.
Something looked at them. It selected four. **The selection took no measurable
time and involved no communication of any kind.**

Of the four: a petty officer, a radioman, a civilian surveyor, and **the cook,
Tsuruoka Masaru**, who had come in to see about the stove.

**The official account** was written in November, three months later, in Tokyo,
by men who were not present. It describes a deliberate contact, a set of
protocols, and an agreement.

**Both documents are in the same cabinet.** Nobody has ever ordered the first
one destroyed. It is simply never cited.

### Tsuruoka

The cook is the programme's permanent problem and its most honest fact.

He had no rank worth recording, no aptitude anyone had noticed, no belief, and
no reason to be in the room. He came in about the stove.

**Every theory of selection the programme has produced in three years has had to
account for Tsuruoka, and none of them has.** The theories get filed anyway.

He is still alive. He still cooks. He was offered a commission in 1942 and
declined it, and the refusal is in his file with no annotation.

## 3. The treaty, and the cabinet it lives in

Nine articles. Beautifully drafted — Amatsu wrote it herself over four nights
and it is genuinely fine legal prose, which is part of what makes it terrible.

**It is written in our hand, on our paper, and nothing has ever signed the other
side.**

It lives in the third cabinet of the Director's office, second drawer, in a
folder with a ribbon. **Shrine-Keeper Petty Officer Hoshino dusts it.**

## 4. The ceremony, which may be doing nothing

Ninety minutes. An officiant, prescribed language, a sequence, a duration, and a
form completed afterward in triplicate.

**Fusions have occurred without it. Fusions have failed to occur with it,
correctly performed, on candidates who wanted it badly.**

The programme continues it in full, every time, and the reason is not belief.
Stopping would be an admission that the Empire has no part in this — that it did
not achieve a contact, negotiate terms, or select its own soldiers, but was
simply **the place where something reached in and took whoever it wanted.**

So: *the ceremony was performed correctly. This appears not to have mattered. The
next one is scheduled.*

**Nobody in the game ever says this is decorative.** The player should notice,
somewhere around the fourth ceremony, that nothing in the Shrine branch has a
mechanism anybody can point to.

## 6. Living with it

**The fused do not discuss the watching with each other.** They can tell who else
has it. They leave it alone, the way people leave a shared illness alone.

It never intensifies and never relents. There is no moment. It is not frightening
while it is happening — it is frightening at three in the morning, in the fourth
month, when you notice you have stopped minding.

**Some of them have started addressing it.** Not aloud, not asking for anything.
Just noting things to it. *That is in no report and it is in several letters.*

### What the station is like

- **Wind, constantly.** The peninsula in winter. Doors that will not stay shut.
- **The sea, which is loud, and which nobody mentions.**
- **The light sweeping the barracks ceiling every eleven seconds, all night**, in
  a building the programme put up without thinking about where the light goes.
  Nobody has asked Ogawa to change the timing. Men lie awake counting it.
- **Very good food**, disproportionately. Tsuruoka is a serious cook and has more
  time than he did. It is the one unambiguous kindness at Light 7 and it is
  administered by the man nobody can explain.

## 7. The turn — Aust

Volume XIV §2. **The watcher selects Helene Aust**, at Hollernbruch, in the
Empire, in 1944.

**What Light 7 knows: nothing, at first.**

Selection has never been announced to the programme. It has always been
discovered — a man reports what happened to him, or somebody notices. **The
programme finds out about its own selections the way a household finds out about
weather.**

So the question that belongs to this faction is not *what did it do*, it is
**how would Light 7 ever learn**, and the answer is the story:

- It does not, for some time.
- Then it does, imperfectly, through an intelligence channel that has no idea
  what it is reporting.
- And then **Amatsu has to decide whether to file it.**

Filing it means the treaty is dead in writing, in a cabinet, where anyone can
read it. Not filing it means she is the only person carrying the fact — and she
already knows the treaty is unsigned, and has carried that alone for three years.

**Do not resolve what she does.** §00. But the shape of the decision is hers and
it is the best scene the faction has.

## 8. Personnel

- **Onmyōji-Taisa Reiko Amatsu.** Wrote the treaty. Knows it is unsigned. Would
  do it again, and her reasoning is the strongest argument in the faction: an
  Empire that admits it is being used has no policy, a programme with no policy
  is disbanded, and disbanding it would not stop a single selection.
- **Captain Ishida.** Believes Article 6 protects them. Has drafted a
  clarification and is waiting for a reply. **He will wait for the whole war.**
  Sincere, decent, entirely wrong, and the most useful viewpoint in the faction.
- **Ogawa**, lighthouse keeper, sixty-eight, no clearance, tends the light.
  Knows more than anyone and nothing at all.
- **Tsuruoka Masaru**, cook, selected 1941, declined a commission in 1942.
- **Sgt Hoshino**, Shrine-Keeper. Dusts the treaty. Knows what he is doing.
- **Sgt Mifune**, karakasa, guards a door.
- **The woman in Kanazawa.** Selected, never inducted, visited twice yearly by an
  officer with a form and four questions. Has never been told what she is. **Has
  begun to work it out.**
- **The one taken in March 1943.** Missing. Presumed lost in the Pacific.
