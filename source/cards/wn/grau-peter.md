## Draw

Twenty-nine, Kadaver, and he admires Aust the way a junior admires
anyone who does the job cleanly and does not explain herself. He has
never read her objections, filed as they are in a drawer that is not
his to open, and he would agree with every one of them, and does not
know this about himself, and will not find out from her.

He signs what she taught him to sign — *adequate to purpose* — about a
door on the north stair he does not believe is adequate to anything,
because the phrase is the one that gets read, and he learned that from
watching her, without ever once being told.

## Story

### WHAT GETS READ

Grau had written *the seal is not adequate to purpose* on the north
stair requisition twice, in his first year, in his own words, and both
times the form had come back from Accounts with the same three
sentences and no action, and by the third submission he had stopped
writing his own words and started writing Aust's, having noticed,
without ever discussing it with her or with anyone, that her phrase
travelled and his did not.

He did not know that she had noticed this too, years before him, and
built a whole method on it, and had come, by the time Grau arrived, to
regard the method as the only language the building actually spoke. He
only knew that *adequate to purpose* went into the file and was read,
in the narrow sense of being logged, and that his own sentences had
simply not existed as far as the building was concerned, and a man who
wants a thing to exist learns quickly which words make it so.

He admired her for this the way he admired her for everything —
quietly, from the distance a junior keeps from a woman twice his age
who outranks him in every way that matters and none of the ways that
are written down — and it did not occur to him that the objections she
filed nineteen times in eight years were written in a different
language entirely, in plain German, in a drawer he had never opened
because it was not his drawer and there was no requisition that would
get him into it.

If he had read them he would have found his own sentence in the third
one, verbatim, from before he learned better — *the seal is not
adequate to purpose* — quoted by Aust as an example of a true thing
correctly stated and correctly ignored, and he would have understood,
all at once, that the woman he had spent three years learning
efficiency from had spent eight years failing at the other thing, on
purpose, in a hand nobody read, because somebody had to keep saying it
even though saying it did nothing, and that this was not the same
lesson as the one he had learned, and that he had learned the wrong
one, or the only one available to him, which was not quite the same
question and he would not have known how to ask it either.

He did not read them. Nobody had given him a reason to, and giving
himself one had never occurred to him as a thing a junior officer did,
and so the drawer stayed closed, and Grau went on writing *adequate to
purpose* on the north stair form each quarter, competently, correctly,
in a hand that got read, admiring a woman he had never once actually
seen at her real work, which was the failing, not the phrase.
