## Draw

Nine years on the Seuche ward, and the man every new intake gets walked
past the north stair by, because somebody has to explain the bread smell
before their own nose does, and it is easier coming from someone who says
*bakery* and believes it enough to say it kindly.

In the same five minutes he also tells them the one thing that will keep
them alive if a six gets loose on his ward — that it will not smell like
the four does — and neither sentence costs him anything to say, because
he stopped being able to tell, some years back, which one is the lie.

## Story

### THE WALK

Every new intake gets the same walk on the first morning, down the main
corridor past the sealed north stair with its three doors that do not
close properly, and Brenner has given it often enough now that he no
longer needs to think about the order the sentences come in, which is
useful, because thinking about the order was the part that used to
trouble him.

He tells them, first, about the smell, before the wind can tell them
itself and get the explanation wrong. *Bakery*, he says, *four hundred
metres upwind, runs at four in the morning, you'll stop noticing it by
Thursday*, and this is true in every particular that can be checked — the
bakery is real, it does run at four, the smell does fade from notice by
about the fourth day, and a new orderly standing in that corridor, catching
the warm yeast under the carbolic for the first time, has no way of
knowing that the sentence he has just been given is doing two jobs at
once, one of them honest.

He does not tell them, on the first morning, what the smell actually is,
because nobody told him on his, and because the ward has a rule about
timing that predates him and that he has never once seen written down: a
man learns what the smell means only after he has learned to live beside
it, since a man who is told both facts in the same week generally asks for
a transfer, and the ward cannot run on transfers.

What he does tell them, in the second week, quietly, on a night shift,
without any of the first morning's warmth, is the thing that is not in
the manual and has never been, because writing it down would mean filing
a document about what a six actually is, and nobody at Hollernbruch wants
to be the name on that form: *a four smells like the bakery. A six does
not smell like anything at all.* He has said this sentence perhaps forty
times in nine years, to forty men standing exactly where the last one
stood, and he has never once had to explain why it matters, because by
the second week they already understand the ward well enough to hear it.

He could not tell you, if the veterinary officer who inspects the kennels
ever thought to ask him, which of the two lessons he considers the real
one — the smell that is not the bakery, or the smell that means run — and
he has stopped trying to work it out for himself sometime around his
fourth year, on the theory that a man who has to choose between the two
kindnesses he is capable of giving a nineteen-year-old on his first
posting has already lost something worth keeping, and Brenner intends to
go on giving both of them, in order, on schedule, for as long as intakes
keep arriving to be walked down that corridor.
