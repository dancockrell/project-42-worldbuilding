## Draw

The earliest retrieval the programme ever attempted, and the least
successful, in every sense the word admits — he speaks to nobody, has
spoken to nobody in three years, and is the only person at Camp Iron Bell
who is visibly, permanently frightened, a condition the file has never
managed to improve on describing beyond noting that it persists.

Nobody has established whether he understands where he is. Nobody has
established it because nobody has found a way to ask that he would
answer, and the programme, having pulled a man out of roughly 1650 into a
century he cannot have any framework for, has never seriously proposed
that the silence might be the correct response to the question rather
than a failure to grasp it.

## Story

### THE GARDEN

The cooks had noticed him first, in the second week, standing at the edge
of the kitchen plot in the grey hour before the mess opened, not doing
anything they could identify as work, simply standing, and had reported
it, mildly, to nobody in particular, the way one reports weather, and had
gone on reporting it, mildly, for several days, until one of them had
thought to leave a spade out, propped against the fence, on no particular
theory, simply because a man standing at the edge of a garden and a spade
left within reach of him seemed, to her, like two facts that ought to be
allowed to meet.

He had begun digging the following morning, in a pattern the cooks did
not recognise and did not interfere with, rows that ran neither with the
fence line nor against it but at an angle that satisfied some logic none
of them could name, and had planted, over the following weeks, things the
installation had not requested and had not been asked to approve, and the
cooks had found, without discussing it among themselves, that they had
begun adjusting the kitchen's requisitions around whatever came up in his
rows, a form of accommodation that had never been authorised by anyone
with the authority to authorise it and had simply happened, the way
useful arrangements at Camp Iron Bell tended to happen, without a form
and therefore without a name.

Nobody had asked him what he was growing, there being no shared language
between the question and any answer he might have given, and the
installation's linguists — such as they were, two men with a smattering
of the period's Dutch between them, assembled from books rather than from
any living speaker — had tried, in his first month, and had produced
nothing, he having simply continued digging while they spoke, in a
silence they had eventually understood as neither hostility nor
incomprehension but something closer to a man declining to participate in
a conversation he had already, privately, concluded would not help either
party.

He worked the garden every morning, without exception, in weather that
kept every other man at the installation indoors, and was, by the third
year, visibly less frightened while doing it than at any other hour of
the day, a fact the cooks had noticed and never reported, on the shared
and unspoken understanding that a fact like that, written down on a form,
would very likely become a fact somebody in authority felt obliged to do
something about, and the garden, as it stood, needed nothing done to it
at all.
