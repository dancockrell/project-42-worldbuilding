## Draw

Presumed lost at Chickamauga in 1863, an artilleryman who cannot read a
word and has never needed to, because he can tell you where an incoming
shell will land before the sound of the gun that fired it reaches him —
an instinct the programme has tried to extract into a manual forty
separate times, from forty separate officers, and has gotten nothing.

Pfc Osei reads him his letters from home, twice through, and has told no
one, because Pike has never once asked him to keep it quiet and neither
of them has ever needed the arrangement explained.

## Story

### THE FORTIETH QUESTION

Lieutenant Cazares had a notebook, a fountain pen, and the particular
patience of a man who believes that if he only found the right order for
his questions, an answer that had refused thirty-nine other orderings
would finally come loose, and he had Pike stand at the edge of the pit
with his eyes shut while a spotter three hundred yards off called the
range, and Pike raised his hand, flat, the way he always did, and said
*short and left*, and it landed short and left, the way it had thirty-nine
times before, near enough that Cazares had stopped bothering to measure
the margin.

"Walk me through it," Cazares said, which was the same request in new
clothes, and Pike opened his eyes and looked at him with the specific,
weary patience of a man being asked, again, to hand over a thing he does
not own in a form he has never possessed, and said what he always said,
which was that he heard it, and Cazares wrote *heard it* in the notebook
under thirty-nine other instances of the same two words, in a column that
by now took up most of a page and explained nothing to anybody who had
not already been standing in the pit.

Pike could not have told him more if the lieutenant had offered him a
commission for it, not from stubbornness, which was the theory the second
or third officer had tried and abandoned, but because there had never been
a sentence in him to begin with — the shell arrived in his body the way
weather arrives in a joint that aches before rain, a fact rather than a
reasoning, and a man cannot be made to explain a fact any more than he can
be made to read one, and Pike, who had never in his life been able to do
the second thing, had stopped expecting anyone to believe he genuinely
could not do the first.

Osei found him afterward behind the ammunition shed with a letter from his
sister in his coat pocket, unopened because opening it accomplished
nothing on his own, and read it to him twice through the way he always
did, once at speed and once slow enough for Pike to stop him and ask about
a word, and neither of them mentioned Cazares or the notebook or the
fortieth failed attempt to write down a thing that had never once, in
Pike's whole life, existed anywhere words could reach it.

"She says the Fentons lost the boy," Osei said, at the second word Pike
had stopped him on, and Pike nodded slowly, the way he nodded at a shell's
report before the shell had landed, and said he'd known it was coming, and
did not say how, because there was, as always, nothing further to say.
