## Draw

The finest metallurgist the Line Shed has ever had, retrieved out of 1923
by a misfiled naturalisation record and a laboratory fire that between
them erased him from a country he had not yet been made to leave, and
delivered, in 1943, into a different country that is at that same moment
building camps for people who share his face and none of his history.

He has not left the wire in eleven months. There is no order that says he
may not. There has never been a meeting at which the question was raised,
a form on which it could have been recorded, or an officer who decided
anything — there is only a fact, unwritten and complete, that he has
understood correctly without needing to be told.

## Story

### THE ROAD HE HAS NOT WALKED

He had asked, in his second week, whether Copiah County had a hardware
supplier closer than the one the requisition officer routinely used,
having noticed, in the inventory, an inefficiency he thought he could
correct with an afternoon and a truck, and Corporal Haines had looked at
him for a moment longer than the question required before saying that she
would check on it, and had not, in fact, checked on it, and had not come
back to him about it either, and Nakada had understood, from the length
of the pause rather than from anything said in it, that he had asked a
question with an answer nobody at the installation wished to be the one
to give him.

He had not asked again. He had noticed, over the following weeks, a
pattern he had not gone looking for and could not, once seen, stop seeing
— that the men who went into Hazlehurst on the supply runs were always
the same several men, that the roster for those runs never included his
name though he had the truck qualification on file, that nobody had ever
told him this was deliberate because, so far as he could determine, it
had never been decided, by anyone, at any meeting, on any form — the camp
had simply arrived, without a single person choosing it, at an
arrangement in which he stayed inside the wire, and the arrangement held,
week after week, for the entirely sufficient reason that nobody had to
choose it twice.

He understood the shape of what he was working inside of more precisely,
he suspected, than most of the men who maintained it without discussing
it, having spent the first quarter of his life in a country that had done
this to other people with considerably less delicacy and rather more
paperwork, and he found, turning the comparison over on the evenings when
turning it over was unavoidable, that the American version disturbed him
in a register the earlier one had not quite managed — not because it was
worse, since it plainly was not, but because it had achieved the same
outcome, a man's world quietly reduced to the size of a fenced compound,
without a single person anywhere in the chain ever having to sign
anything, decide anything, or say out loud what they were doing, which
meant there was, when he looked for one, no name he could put to the
decision and no officer he could in fairness hold it against, only an
installation that had, collectively and blamelessly, arrived at his
confinement the way water arrives at the low point of a field.

Chief Warrant Officer Deel told anyone who would listen that Nakada was
the best metallurgist the Line Shed had ever had, and meant it, and
believed, saying it, that he was giving the man his due. Nakada had heard
him say it three times and had thanked him, correctly, on each occasion,
and had not yet decided whether the compliment and the eleven months
inside the wire were two separate facts about the installation or the
same fact, stated twice, in two registers, by people who could not have
told you, if you had asked either of them plainly, which one they meant.
