## Story

### THE CHAIR BY THE DOOR

He had started sitting nearer the door sometime in the spring, without
deciding to, the way a man finds himself favouring one side of a bed
after his wife has been gone from it for some months, an adjustment made
by the body before the mind has agreed to it, and he had not connected
the habit to anything in particular until the evening in June when Pfc
Okonkwo had come into the mess looking for him, had walked the length of
the room twice, and had apologised, on finding him, for not having seen
him the first time, a small and ordinary thing to say that Reyes-Whitlock
had turned over afterward with more attention than it likely deserved,
noting that Okonkwo had not said the room was dim, or that he had been
distracted, or any of the usual courtesies a man offers when he has
simply failed to look properly — he had said *I didn't see you*, flatly,
as a fact about the room rather than about his own attention, and Reyes-
Whitlock, who had by then spent a considerable portion of eleven years
learning to read the precise weight people put on such phrases, had heard
in it something closer to an apology for an oversight the speaker did not
fully understand himself.

He had asked Falk, once, directly, what her honest reading of him was,
setting aside the tables, and she had not answered him, that time or the
second time he asked, some weeks later, not from unkindness — he did not
think she was capable of unkindness in a medical capacity, having watched
her be careful with him in every other particular — but from what he had
come to interpret as a physician's specific reluctance to put into words
a measurement she did not believe she could take accurately, there being,
so far as either of them knew, no instrument built for the question he
was actually asking, which was not *how far along am I* but *is there a
floor to this*, a question the coherence tables did not address because
the coherence tables had been written by people who had never imagined a
man reaching a point past their bottom line.

He did not raise the question a third time. He had noticed that he
minded the silence less than he had expected to, which he took to be
either a form of peace or a form of the thing itself progressing, and had
found that he could no longer, with confidence, tell the two apart, and
had stopped trying to, on the grounds that a man who spends his remaining
attention distinguishing acceptance from disappearance has, in a
meaningful sense, already answered the question he was so carefully not
asking.

He kept the chair by the door. It gave him, he found, a clean line to
the corridor, and he told himself this was practical, and did not examine
further why a man with nowhere in particular to be had begun, of late,
to prefer a seat positioned for an exit.
