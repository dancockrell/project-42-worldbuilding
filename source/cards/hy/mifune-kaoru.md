## Draw

Volunteered, and was one of the few volunteers ever taken — and received,
of everything the folklore catalogue holds, the smallest entry in it. He
guards a door. Nothing has passed him in two years, which is exactly the
problem: he asked for something enormous and was handed something useful.

There was no ceremony prepared for a disappointing fusion. One was held
anyway, in full, correctly, and he stood through all ninety minutes of it
without once saying what he had actually asked for.

## Story

### THE DOOR

He had asked, at the ceremony, for something he did not say aloud even to
himself in the asking, only felt the shape of — largeness, weight, a
folklore that would announce itself the way Kurosawa's drum announced
itself, or the way the whistlers announced themselves before anyone saw
them — and had knelt through the Reading and the Interval and the Naming
with that shape held carefully in front of him, the way a man holds a
cupped flame he is not yet permitted to look at directly, and had received,
when the officiant finally named it, a small, common ward against passage,
the kind of protection a farmhouse gate might have carried in an older
century, and had understood, kneeling there in front of the entire
detachment, that the ceremony was continuing exactly as scripted around a
disappointment nobody present was going to acknowledge as one.

He had not said what he asked for. He had decided this within the same
minute he received the news, on the reasoning that naming the size of the
thing he had wanted would only sharpen, for everyone watching, the size of
what he had gotten instead, and a man could survive being handed something
small a great deal more easily than he could survive an audience's precise
understanding of how small it was next to his hope.

What he had not accounted for, in two years of guarding the same door, was
that he would become good at it — not adequately, competently good, the
kind of good that gets mentioned in a report and forgotten, but genuinely,
measurably good, nothing having passed him in two years of nights,
including once a fox that had no business testing a ward at all and had
tested it anyway and found it held — and that this competence, rather than
consoling him, had become the exact shape of the humiliation he had been
trying to spare himself at the ceremony: a man who fails at something small
can tell himself the folklore was wrong for him. A man who succeeds at it,
night after night, has no such comfort available, because the folklore is
plainly working exactly as intended, and the only failure left to locate
is the size of what he had wanted in the first place.

Ishida had told him once, early on, that Article VI protected men in his
position, and Mifune had believed it, briefly and gratefully, the way a
man accepts any comfort offered in the first month of a disappointment he
has not yet learned to carry. He had stopped believing it some months
later, without deciding to and without telling anyone, including Ishida,
who continued periodically to mention the article's protections in the
same reassuring tone he always used, never once noticing that the sergeant
guarding the door had quietly stopped needing, or wanting, to hear it.
