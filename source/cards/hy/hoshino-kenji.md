## Draw

One of the four the fusion chose from the signal hut in August 1941, and
the only one who has read both accounts of it — the first-hand notes and
the official version written three months later by men who were not
there — in the same afternoon, in the same cabinet, and put them back in
the order he found them.

He dusts the treaty every week. He has never told Amatsu he read the other
file. She has never asked. They have had eleven careful conversations
since, and neither has said about what.

## Story

### THE ORDER HE FOUND THEM IN

He had gone into the cabinet for the dusting cloth, which was kept on the
second shelf behind the folders rather than in front of them, an
arrangement nobody had explained and he had never questioned, and his hand
had found, in reaching past the treaty, a second folder he had not known
was there, thinner, undated, and had opened it the way a man opens a
drawer he has opened a hundred times and for once finds something new
in — not suspicious yet, only curious — and had read, standing there with
the dusting cloth still in his other hand, the first-hand notes from
August, three disagreeing accounts in three hands he did not recognise,
none of them matching the version he had been told, and had then read the
official version beside it, written three months later in Tokyo by men who
had not been present, and had understood, closing both folders and
returning them to precisely the shelf and precisely the order he had found
them in, that he now knew something Amatsu had chosen not to give him, and
that she likely knew he had access to the cabinet, and that neither fact
obliged him to do anything at all.

He had dusted the treaty on schedule the following week, as he had every
week for three years, and Amatsu had been in the room for part of it,
signing something at her desk, and had said nothing about the cabinet, and
he had said nothing about the cabinet, and this had not felt, to either of
them, like an omission, because an omission requires a question that went
unasked, and neither had asked anything.

In the eleven conversations they had had since — routine ones, transfers
and rosters and the state of the shrine paint — he had watched her for any
sign that she knew what he now knew, and had found only the same
precision she showed everyone, which told him nothing, which he took,
after the third or fourth conversation, to be its own kind of answer:
a woman who drafted nine articles of careful legal prose over four nights
did not accidentally leave a second folder within reach of a man who
dusted the cabinet weekly, and if she had left it there on purpose, the
purpose was not his to guess at, and if it had been an oversight, correcting
it now would only tell her what she perhaps did not yet need to know.

He answered every question anyone at Light Seven put to him, fully and
precisely, and had never once, in three years, been the one to raise a
question of his own, which the others took for a kind of incuriosity and
which was, in fact, the opposite — a man who has read both accounts of his
own selection and found neither of them adequate has a great many
questions, and has simply decided, in the particular stillness with which
he closes a cabinet drawer, which ones are worth the asking.
