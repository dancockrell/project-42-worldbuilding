## Draw

A schoolteacher before the war and still one in every way that matters.
Runs the programme's only genuine attempt at systematic record-keeping
about the fused, and is compiling the twelfth theory of selection knowing
it will fail, because eleven failures is data and twelve is more data.

She has requested Ueda's original account, four years of the Kanazawa
visit records, and — unusually, and to nobody's understanding but her
own — the file on the two hundred men and women who volunteered and were
never chosen.

## Story

### THE FILE ON THE UNCHOSEN

The clerk who brought her the requisition slip had read it twice before
filing it, on the reasonable suspicion that Lieutenant Sakaguchi had meant
to write *the file on the chosen* and simply transposed the word, since
every theory the programme had produced in three years concerned itself
with the four in the signal hut and the thirty-seven added since, and none
had ever once asked after the roughly two hundred who had volunteered and
received nothing — men and women who existed in the records only as a
subtraction, the number left over once the interesting figure had been
removed — and he had gone back to her office to confirm the request rather
than risk the delay of a wrong file, and she had told him, without looking
up from the theory she was drafting, that no, she had meant precisely what
she had written.

She had explained nothing further, which the clerk took, correctly, as an
answer rather than an evasion, since Sakaguchi did not withhold conclusions
out of secrecy so much as out of a schoolteacher's old habit of not
supplying an answer a student might yet arrive at honestly, and she had
in fact arrived at nothing conclusive herself, only a suspicion she was
not prepared to write into a file before she had tested it, on the
reasoning — sound, and entirely her own, and shared with no one, not even
Amatsu, who had approved the requisition without asking what it was for —
that eleven theories had failed for the same unexamined reason: each had
looked exclusively at what the selected had in common, and none had
thought to ask what the *unselected* had in common instead, on the
assumption, never stated because never questioned, that a population that
received nothing could not be evidence of anything.

She did not yet know whether the two hundred would tell her anything. She
suspected, reading the first dozen files that evening by lamp, that they
might tell her a great deal, and that what they told her would not be a
pattern in who was chosen but a pattern in who volunteered in the first
place, which was a different question from the one anyone had asked in
three years, and possibly, she thought, closing the twelfth file and
opening the thirteenth, the only question that had ever been worth asking.

She wrote none of this into the theory that evening. The twelfth would
fail like the other eleven — she was nearly certain of it, and had said
so, once, to no one — and she intended it to fail on schedule, filed
correctly, its negative result as carefully documented as any positive one
would have been, because a failure recorded honestly was still data, and
data, unlike belief, could be returned to later by someone who finally knew
what to look for in it.
