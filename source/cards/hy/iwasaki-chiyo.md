## Draw

Selected in 1942 and never told, never inducted, never trained — folklore
chosen her the way it chose the rest of the roster, and then did nothing
else about it, leaving a shopkeeper who mends other people's kimono to
work out, alone, from four repeated questions, what she is.

An officer visits twice a year with a form. She has answered *no* to all
four questions since the second visit. She is lying every time, and she is
the only person in this transaction who knows it is a lie.

## Story

### THE FOURTH QUESTION

The lieutenant who came to Kanazawa twice a year was, by 1943, a young man
she had watched turn middle-aged in four visits, the way you notice a
neighbour's child has grown only when you have not seen the child in
between, and he asked her, each time, in the same order, whether she had
been sleeping, whether she had been unwell, whether anything unusual had
occurred, and whether she wished to report anything, and each time she
folded her hands in her lap in the manner of a woman with nothing to
report and said no, no, no, no, and he wrote the four characters down in a
hand that had also aged, and thanked her, and left, and this had gone on
long enough now that she had begun to think of it the way she thought of
the plum tree in the shop's courtyard, which also came around twice a
year, reliably, asking nothing of her but to be noticed.

She had worked out, somewhere in the second year, that the questions were
wrong for a civilian — that no army sends an officer a full day's train
journey twice a year to ask a shopkeeper about her sleep, that the fourth
question in particular, *whether she wished to report anything*, was not a
kindness but a door left open on purpose, by someone who both hoped and
dreaded that she would one day walk through it — and once she had worked
this out she found she could not un-know it, the way a woman who has
noticed a single loose thread in a fine weave can never again look at that
sleeve without her eye finding the thread first, and so she had begun, from
the third visit onward, to lie, carefully, in the full knowledge that she
was lying, to a man who she had also begun to suspect knew that she was
lying, and who wrote down her lie anyway, in the same careful hand, four
times a year, twice.

She had a theory about the fourth question, and she believed it correctly:
that somewhere above the lieutenant there was an office that could not order
her told what she was, and could not order the visits stopped either,
because stopping them would be its own kind of telling, and so it had
settled, the way such offices settle, on asking her forever whether she
wished to volunteer the truth she had not been given — the single most
Japanese Army solution available to a problem the Japanese Army did not know
how to name.

What she did not have a theory for was what would happen the day she
answered yes, and she had come close only twice, once in the loneliness of
the second year and once on a night she did not examine closely even alone,
and both times had felt the *no* form on her tongue before she had decided
to say it, the way a hand that has darned the same tear every winter for
years finds the stitch before the mind remembers the pattern. She understood,
without ever framing it as understanding, that the lie she told the
lieutenant twice a year had become the only room in her life large enough
to hold both of them, and that the day either told the truth in it the room
would be gone, and so would he, and possibly so would she, and neither of
them, four visits running, had been willing to be the one who tried the
door.
