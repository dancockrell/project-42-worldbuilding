## Draw

A fisherman from Wajima, forty-one, taken from the same boat that carried
her brother — he was in it, she was in it, and the fusion chose her and
left him standing. He transferred to Light Seven as unchosen support two
years ago rather than be parted from her again.

They work the same shift and eat at the same table, perfectly friendly,
and have not discussed the boat once in two years. Neither of them has
ever said which of them is the one avoiding it.

## Story

### THE SAME TABLE

They had eaten across from each other perhaps six hundred times since he
transferred, by her rough count, which she had never shared with him
because sharing it would have required explaining why she had bothered to
keep it, and in all six hundred meals the boat had come up exactly once,
obliquely, when a new arrival at the mess had asked, in the friendly and
tactless way of someone who did not yet know the shape of the silences at
Light Seven, whether the two of them had always fished together, and her
brother had said yes, and she had said yes, and the new arrival had asked
nothing further, and the two of them had gone back to eating with the
particular carefulness of people who have just watched a door swing open
and shut again on its own, unbothered by anyone's hand.

She had decided, in the first weeks after his transfer came through, that
he had followed her out of guilt, and had further decided that the
kindest thing available to her was to give him nothing to be guilty
about — no scene, no accusation, no moment in which she named what had
happened in the boat and made him sit with having survived it — and she
had held to this so consistently, for two years, that she had stopped
being entirely sure whether it was kindness any longer or simply the
easiest available shape for two people who ate three meals a day within
arm's reach of a fact neither could put down.

He, for his part, had decided the same thing from the opposite side,
having concluded within a month of his transfer that raising the boat
would only ask her to comfort him for a thing that had happened to her,
which struck him as an obscene use of anyone's time, let alone hers, and
had settled instead into being reliably, undemandingly present — the same
shift, the same table, a hand that passed the rice before she had to ask
for it — on the theory that a brother who required nothing was worth more
to her than one who required her forgiveness.

Neither of them had ever compared these two private accounts of the same
two years, and had they done so, each would have discovered the other had
been protecting them from an examination neither of them, in fact, wanted
to conduct, which might have been the single most useful conversation
available to either of them and which neither, six hundred meals in, had
found a way to begin — not from fear, exactly, but from the settled and
mutual understanding that whichever one spoke first would be asking the
other to answer for something that had never been anyone's fault to
answer for, and asking that of a person you loved was, they had each
separately concluded, the one thing worse than the silence.
