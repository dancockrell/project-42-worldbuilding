## Draw

Says the opposite of what is wanted, and has been right often enough to
keep — eleven contradicted orders, eleven correct contradictions, filed
under a category the establishment has quietly decided to call
*consultative* rather than the word the folklore actually uses, which is
*perverse*. Nobody has worked out whether he chooses this. He would like,
just once, to agree with somebody, and has not yet managed it — not from
stubbornness, but because the part of him that answers no longer consults
him before it speaks.

## Story

### ELEVEN, SO FAR

The colonel had asked him directly, after the ninth contradiction had
turned out correct and cost the colonel a night's sleep working out how
to write the after-action report without crediting a private for
overruling him, whether he did it on purpose, and he had answered
honestly, which was that he did not know, and had watched the colonel
decide, visibly, that an honest *I don't know* from a man who was right
nine times out of nine was more useful to keep than a confident liar
would have been, and had been reassigned, the following week, to stand
near every briefing rather than march in every column, which he had
understood immediately as a promotion dressed as a convenience.

What he had not managed to explain to the colonel, or to anyone, because
he had not managed to explain it to himself first, was that the
contradiction did not arrive to him as a considered opinion he then
voiced. It arrived the way a sneeze arrives — already in motion by the
time he noticed it starting, already shaped into a sentence before the
part of him that might have agreed with the room had finished forming its
agreement — and he had learned, watching himself do this eleven times
now, that the two halves of him disagreed with each other at every single
briefing and that only one of them was ever permitted to speak.

He had wanted, more than once, to be the halfway man again — to hear a
plan, find it sound, and simply say so, the way every other soldier in
the tent was free to do without a folklore reaching into his throat first
— and had tried it twice, deliberately, biting down on the objection
before it left him and letting the room's consensus stand unchallenged,
and both times the operation had gone forward as planned and both times,
within two days, something had gone wrong that his silenced objection
would have named in advance, and he had stopped trying after the second
one, not because he had proof the contradiction was infallible, but
because he had run out of nerve to test it against men's lives a third
time.

He stood now near the tent flap during the evening briefing, listening to
a plan he privately, entirely, agreed with, and felt the familiar
pressure building somewhere behind his sternum, the shape of a
contradiction assembling itself whether he wanted it to or not, and
thought, with the specific tiredness of a man watching a storm he cannot
outrun approach from a direction he can already name, *not this one, just
this once, let this one be right as it stands* — and opened his mouth,
and said no.
