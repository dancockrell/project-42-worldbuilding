## Draw

Selected in 1943 and fused with something the folklore calls Oni — he does
not remember agreeing to it, only a gap the shape of a held breath — and
given, in exchange, the one thing no other fused person at Light Seven has:
a real ending, and a drum that tells him when it is close.

He has learned to read two rhythms in himself. The first makes the men
around him fight harder. He has played it nine times. The second means
there will not be enough of him left afterward to need the drum back. He
has not played it yet, and he writes to his sister as though he never will.

## Story

### NINE LETTERS, NONE OF THEM SENT

He had written to his sister nine times since the ceremony, always at
night, always by the lamp that made the drum's shadow too large for the
wall it fell on, and he had learned, by the third letter, to open with the
food, because the food was true and pleasant and required nothing further
of him — the cook fed them better than the ration books would have
predicted, on account of being one of them and refusing, in some manner
nobody had asked him to explain, to act like it — and a man who has begun a
letter well can often carry the good beginning a long way before the letter
notices what it is actually about.

She had asked, in the New Year, whether he was coming home for it, and he
had not answered the question in that letter and had told himself, folding
it into the drawer with the others, that he would answer it properly once
he knew more, which was a way of saying he would answer it once he no
longer needed to lie, which was a way of saying he expected never to answer
it at all. He had written, instead, that the drum was fine, that she would
say it was too loud, that it was exactly loud enough — three sentences he
was fairly certain were true and knew for certain were not the sentence she
had asked for, and he had chosen them anyway, the way a man crossing a
narrow bridge chooses to look at his feet rather than the water, not out of
cowardice exactly, but because looking at the water changed nothing about
the crossing and only made the crossing worse.

What he had, and what none of the others at Light Seven had, was a genuine
choice, arriving twice a battle in the shape of a rhythm his own hands
found before his mind caught up to them — the first, which cost him
something and left him standing, and the second, which would not — and he
had come to think of this choice the way a man thinks of money he intends
never to spend: real, his own, and worth more sitting untouched in a
drawer than converted into anything he might actually have to explain to
his sister. He had played the first rhythm nine times and had felt, each
time, a small and private pride that he did not examine too closely, the
pride of a man managing a debt correctly.

He did not know, writing the ninth letter, that there would be a tenth. He
knew only that spring was a real season that would genuinely arrive, and
that he had promised her something in it, and that promising a true season
was the closest he had come, in a year of lying to her by omission, to
telling her something he actually believed.
